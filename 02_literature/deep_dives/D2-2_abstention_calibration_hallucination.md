# Abstention, calibration and hallucination — deep dive (S2-3)

## 0. Scope and method

I searched with 34 English title-phrase queries against the arXiv index through the project's verification tool, plus targeted web lookups for dataset cards and ACL Anthology records. Queries were deliberately of two kinds: canonical anchors ("selective classification for deep neural networks", "on calibration of modern neural networks", "know what you don't know unanswerable questions for SQuAD") and adversarial probes ("do LLMs really know what they don't know", "answerability in retrieval-augmented open-domain question answering", "two axes of LLM abstention"). Roughly 110 candidate records were screened by title and year; 41 were resolved to a real record and written to the staging CSV, and every one of those 41 is cited below. I read abstracts for all 41, and abstracts plus results sections for nine: the AbstentionBench paper, the two-axes paper, the 2025 "unsaid vs. unrecognised" line, the RAG answerability paper, UAEval4RAG, Selective QA under Domain Shift, A-CRC-QA, the post-hoc confidence-estimator paper, and the 2025 semantic-entropy-probe critique.

Two method notes. First, the arXiv API returned sustained 429s for long stretches (the batch is running seven agents on one IP), so several claims were resolved through the 10.48550 DOI path against OpenAlex/Crossref instead; the CSV records which command resolved each row. Second, this area has an unusually high ratio of benchmark-construction artefacts to real findings, so Section 3 is written as a critique of measurement rather than a summary of methods.

## 1. Taxonomy

### 1.1 Risk–coverage and selective prediction

The founding move is to stop treating abstention as a classifier output and start treating it as a *budget*. A selective classifier is allowed to reject, and the quantity of interest is the risk (error rate) on the accepted set as a function of coverage. Geifman and El-Yaniv's method gives the user a target risk level and selects a threshold at test time that meets it with high probability; on ImageNet they guarantee 2% top-5 error at 99.9% confidence while retaining almost 60% coverage [1]. SelectiveNet moved the reject option inside training, optimising classification and rejection jointly rather than thresholding a pre-trained model's confidence [2]. For NLP, the ACL 2021 study found that larger pre-trained transformers improve accuracy and confidence quality together, and proposed a cheap error-regularisation trick that improves the confidence estimator without much extra compute [3].

The most deflationary paper in this cluster is the 2023 study of post-hoc confidence estimators over 84 ImageNet classifiers. It observed that many networks have a "broken" confidence estimator whose selective performance is far worse than their accuracy would predict, and showed that normalising the logits by their p-norm and then taking the max logit largely repairs it — after which selective performance is almost entirely determined by accuracy, and the ordering is stable under distribution shift [4]. That is a warning for anyone planning to publish a clever abstention head: on well-behaved classifiers, most of the headroom is a normalisation constant.

The one paper in the batch that reports the trade-off the way a deployment engineer would want is the domain-shift QA study: a calibrator trained to predict "the QA model will err here" answered 56% of questions at 80% accuracy, against 48% for thresholding the model's own softmax, precisely because softmax confidence is overconfident out of domain [5]. Conformal methods supply the guarantees this literature keeps promising. Risk-controlling prediction sets give finite-sample control of expected loss for a user-specified, cost-weighted loss [6]; conformal language modelling calibrates a sampling-and-rejection procedure so that the returned candidate set contains an acceptable answer with high probability [7]; and A-CRC-QA applies a monotonised empirical-risk procedure to selective QA, reporting that instance-wise loss is generally non-monotone in the threshold and that only asymptotic, not finite-sample, control is available [8]. That last caveat is the honest version of the claim, and it matters: it means the guarantee a workshop paper can defensibly claim is weaker than the guarantee readers will assume.

### 1.2 Calibration: measurement first, repair second

The modern literature starts from the empirical fact that deeper, wider, batch-normalised networks are badly calibrated, and that temperature scaling — one parameter — fixes most of it [9]. The measurement literature then took that clean story apart. A systematic study of ECE variants showed that the choice of norm (L1 vs L2), binning scheme, and whether you condition on class changes not just the number but the *rank ordering of recalibration methods* [10]. If the ranking of methods depends on the metric, then cross-paper ECE comparisons are close to meaningless. The same lesson appears from the estimation side: popular scaling methods are less calibrated than reported and cannot estimate their own error, while histogram binning is measurable but needs O(B/ε²) samples rather than O(1/ε²), motivating a hybrid scaling-binning calibrator [11].

For transformers specifically, BERT and RoBERTa turn out to be reasonably calibrated in domain, with out-of-domain calibration error as much as 3.5× lower than strong baselines, temperature scaling helping in domain, and label smoothing helping out of domain [12]. The general claim behind these results is that multi-domain calibration is a trainable surrogate for out-of-domain generalisation, and under stated conditions it provably excludes spurious-correlation solutions [13]. For our purposes the operational lesson is that calibration is a property of a *distribution pair*, not of a model; every number needs its shift named.

### 1.3 Verbalised confidence

Asking a model "how sure are you" is the cheapest possible uncertainty interface, and the evidence is genuinely split by regime. GPT-3, prompted to emit a confidence level in words, produced probabilities that were well calibrated and stayed moderately calibrated under distribution shift [14]. But the models people actually deploy are RLHF-tuned, and the systematic evaluation of elicitation strategies on ChatGPT, GPT-4 and Claude begins from the premise that RLHF models produce badly calibrated conditional probabilities and asks which elicitation strategy recovers usable confidence [15]. A broader empirical comparison of black-box and white-box elicitation found that white-box methods are better but the gap is narrow — 0.605 versus 0.522 AUROC — that no method consistently wins, and that all of them degrade on tasks needing professional knowledge [16]. The SelfAware work is the best-known demonstration that models have *some* self-knowledge — 20 LLMs, five categories of unanswerable question, clear improvement from in-context learning and instruction tuning, but still far below human ability to recognise the limits of knowledge [17]. R-Tuning adds the constructive result that refusal-aware instruction tuning on the knowledge boundary also improves calibration, which is the strongest evidence that refusal and calibration are the same underlying skill [18].

### 1.4 Internal-signal probes

The premise here is that the model's hidden state knows more than its output. Kadavath et al. showed that larger models are well calibrated on multiple-choice and true/false questions in the right format, and that P(True) — the probability the model assigns to its own sampled answer being correct — is usefully calibrated and improves with scale and with access to multiple samples [19]. Semantic entropy replaces sampling-based estimates with a probe: semantic entropy costs 5–10× more compute than a single generation, so semantic entropy probes approximate it directly from the hidden states of one pass [20]. Lookback Lens uses an even simpler feature — the ratio of attention mass on the provided context versus newly generated tokens, per head — and finds that a linear classifier on those ratios matches richer detectors for contextual hallucination [21]. The 2025 work on reasoning models reframes the whole cluster: large reasoning models appear to *recognise* that a question is defective while still answering it, a misalignment between internal cognition and external response, which a two-stage monitoring-plus-intervention method can partially repair [22].

The counter-paper is the most important thing I read in this cluster. It argues that hidden states mainly encode *whether the model is recalling parametric knowledge*, not whether the output is true; it separates unassociated hallucinations (no parametric grounding) from associated hallucinations (driven by spurious associations), and shows that the latter have hidden-state geometry overlapping factual outputs, so standard probes fail on exactly the errors that matter [23]. Any proposal built on "a probe on hidden states" has to answer this paper.

### 1.5 Benchmarks of unanswerability, and the mirror image of over-refusal

SQuAD 2.0 is the anchor: over 50,000 unanswerable questions written adversarially by crowdworkers to look like answerable ones, explicitly to defeat the earlier practice of generating unanswerables that were easy to spot [24]. PopQA supplies the opposite stress: 14k open-domain questions stratified by entity popularity, showing that models fail on the long tail and that scale does not fix memorisation of rare facts, while retrieval beats models orders of magnitude larger [25]. KUQ is a self-detection method rather than a dataset: paraphrase the question, collect answers, and use their divergence as the "I don't know" signal, with no external resources [26].

AbstentionBench is the 2025 consolidation: 20 datasets spanning unknown answers, underspecification, false premises, subjectivity and stale information, 20 frontier models, scored by an LLM judge. Its two headline findings are that scale barely helps and that reasoning fine-tuning *degrades* abstention by about 24% on average, including in maths and science [27]. On the retrieval side, UAEval4RAG defines six categories of unanswerable query, synthesises them against any knowledge base, and reports two metrics — unanswered ratio and acceptable ratio — while sweeping retrieval models, rewriters, rerankers, generators and prompts [28]. The mirror image is over-refusal: XSTest's 250 safe prompts across ten types plus 200 unsafe contrasts showed that models refuse safe prompts that merely resemble unsafe ones, a pattern the authors attribute to lexical overfitting [29]; OR-Bench scales that up with 80,000 automatically rewritten "seemingly toxic" prompts, a 1,000-prompt hard subset, and 600 toxic controls, and finds the safety/over-refusal trade-off roughly monotone across model families [30].

### 1.6 The RAG-specific line

Two papers define the problem. The first shows that answerability classifiers trained the usual way — pair the question with a *random* passage — collapse when the irrelevant passage has high semantic overlap: accuracy falls from 98% to 1%, and training on SQuAD 2.0's unanswerable pairs restores it to near 100% [31]. This is the cleanest published demonstration that "the model can detect unanswerable questions" is mostly a statement about how the negatives were sampled. The second asks whether retrieval-augmented models know when they don't know, and finds a failure mode nobody predicted: when *all* retrieved documents are irrelevant, these models still refuse questions they could have answered correctly — over-refusal driven by retrieval state, not by knowledge state — and that better refusal behaviour does not imply better calibration [32]. UAEval4RAG completes the picture by showing that retriever, rewriter, reranker and prompt choices all move the unanswered/acceptable trade-off — refusal is a pipeline property, not a model property [28].

### 1.7 Hallucination evaluation and its judges

HaluEval generated 35,000 hallucinated/normal sample pairs (5k human-annotated ChatGPT responses plus 30k task-specific pairs for QA, dialogue and summarisation) using a sampling-then-filtering pipeline [33]. RAGTruth is the human-labelled alternative: nearly 18,000 naturally generated RAG responses with word-level hallucination annotations, showing that a fine-tuned 13B detector can match GPT-4-prompted detection [34]. FActScore decomposed long-form generations into atomic facts and measured the fraction supported by a knowledge source, reporting 58% for ChatGPT, and — the part that matters for feasibility — an automated estimator within 2% of human scoring, which is what makes a 6,500-generation study possible at all [35]. TruthfulQA's 817 questions across 38 categories established the awkward scaling result that larger models were *less* truthful, with the best model at 58% against 94% for humans [36].

All of this rests on judges, whose agreement with humans is high but whose biases are documented and structured [37][38]; the abstention survey maps the area and is explicit that evaluation metrics are its weak part [39].

## 2. What is saturated

Four claims are now made often enough that another instance adds nothing. First, "a probe/attention feature/entropy variant detects hallucination at AUROC X on dataset Y": there are dozens, the numbers cluster in the 0.7–0.85 band, and the 2025 hidden-state critique suggests many of them are detecting knowledge recall rather than truth [23]. Second, "verbalised confidence is miscalibrated" — established, with the interesting open question being *which regime* (base vs RLHF) rather than whether [15]. Third, the ECE-variant zoo: after the demonstration that metric choice reorders methods, adding another ECE variant is a measurement hobby, not a contribution [10]. Fourth, "abstention improves safety / reduces hallucination", asserted without a cost model, appears in the introduction of nearly every paper in the area.

One more thing is saturated in a way that flatters the field: SQuAD 2.0 unanswerable accuracy. Numbers in the high 80s are routinely reported as evidence of answerability reasoning, but the unanswerables were written by workers looking at the same paragraph and are, by construction, rich in lexical overlap with the question — the exact feature that makes them detectable by shallow heuristics and the exact feature that fails to transfer [31].

## 3. What is genuinely open

### 3.1 Who actually reports both directions

Asking "does the gate refuse the unanswerable questions" is only half a measurement; the other half is "how many answerable questions does it destroy". Very few papers report both at a single operating point.

The exceptions I can name precisely. Selective QA under Domain Shift is two-sided by construction — it fixes accuracy at 80% and reports coverage, so a gate that refuses everything scores 0% coverage [5]. AbstentionBench puts average accuracy and average abstention recall in the same table for all 20 models [27], which is the most useful aggregate I found, though it is aggregated across datasets and therefore hides the per-dataset precision/recall trade-off. UAEval4RAG reports the unanswered ratio and the acceptable ratio as separate headline metrics, which is literally the two-sided measurement [28]. The two-axes paper goes furthest: it builds a policy in which an answerability score and a correctness score must each clear a separately calibrated threshold, and reports that this certifies both budgets at 0.75 coverage of correct answers versus 0.31 for a single threshold [40]. And the retrieval-refusal paper measures over-refusal on answerable questions *and* refusal on unanswerable ones, which is how it discovered the irrelevant-retrieval over-refusal effect [32].

Everything else is one-directional. SelfAware, KUQ and R-Tuning report accuracy on unanswerable sets with answerable-set accuracy as a side note; XSTest and OR-Bench report refusal rates on safe prompts with the unsafe contrast as a sanity check; the hallucination benchmarks report detection AUROC without a coverage axis at all. The practical consequence is that reported "abstention rates" are not comparable across papers: a 70% abstention rate is excellent if the base rate of unanswerable questions is 50% and catastrophic if it is 5%.

### 3.2 Near-miss versus nonsense: the probe-construction confound

This is the single largest methodological hole, and it is quantifiable. The RAG answerability paper trained a classifier on random negative passages and measured 98% accuracy on random negatives; on negatives with high semantic overlap, the same model scored 1% [31]. A 97-point swing produced entirely by how the negatives were sampled, with no change to the model or the question distribution.

The same structure recurs everywhere. AbstentionBench's per-scenario numbers are suggestive: for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, abstention recall is 0.79 on "answer unknown" but 0.53 on false premise and 0.38 on MMLU-History [27]. Missing-fact questions ("who is the current CEO of X" where X has no CEO) are near-misses only in a weak sense — the entity is absent, which is detectable from retrieval features; false-premise questions require evaluating whether a stated relation holds, which is not. A gate evaluated mostly on the first type will look excellent and generalise to nothing.

The two-axes paper makes the mechanism explicit: answer-confidence is nearly blind to answerability, a hidden-state probe handles answerability but not correctness, and on naturally occurring false-premise questions every verbalised signal — P(IK), P(True), even asking outright whether the premise is false — sits near chance while the probe reaches 0.69–0.77 AUROC [40]. Then the punchline: instructing the model to check premises produces 57% false challenges, because it disputes sound and false premises alike. That number should be quoted in every over-refusal discussion.

So the honest experimental design has a *hardness ladder* for negatives — random passage, same-entity wrong-passage, same-topic near-miss, premise violation, underspecified — and reports the gate's precision and recall at each rung. I did not find a paper that does this as a designed ablation. Papers report one rung and generalise.

### 3.3 Calibration after retrieval and after shift

Calibration papers measure ECE on a held-out split, then on a named OOD set [13]. The abstention literature inherits the habit without the discipline: nobody reports the calibration of the *gate* before and after retrieval changes the feature distribution.

What exists is adjacent. Selective QA under Domain Shift shows softmax confidence is overconfident out of domain and that a trained calibrator can recover eight points of coverage at fixed accuracy [5]. The RALM refusal paper shows the retrieval state itself moves refusal behaviour — irrelevant documents trigger over-refusal — but explicitly concludes that uncertainty estimation for retrieval-augmented models remains open and that refusal quality and calibration quality are not the same axis [32]. The post-hoc confidence paper is the only one that tests its repair under distribution shift, and it finds the *ordering* stable, which is reassuring for images and untested for retrieval [4].

The measurement nobody does: risk–coverage curves for the gate estimated separately on questions answerable from the retrieved context, answerable only from parametric memory, and genuinely unanswerable, with the retrieval budget (k, reranker on/off, corpus) varied as the shift axis. That is one afternoon of compute on a fixed model and it would tell us whether the threshold transfers at all — which is exactly the failure mode our own memory system hit, where a threshold sat outside the observed feature range and over-refused on answerable questions (private, contaminated evaluation; no figure quoted).

### 3.4 Does anyone gate on retrieval features with a two-sided threshold?

The specific design I was asked to check for: features from the retrieval stack (dense similarity, lexical overlap, rerank score, retriever agreement), two thresholds fitted on dev — one to admit, one to refuse, dead zone between — validated on held-out data, with both abstain precision and abstain recall reported.

Nobody does this cleanly, and I want to be precise about how close the nearest work is.

The answerability-in-RAG paper is the closest on features: it trains a classifier over question–passage pairs to detect irrelevant excerpts, and it is the paper that documents the overlap failure [31]. But it reports classification accuracy on constructed negatives, not a calibrated two-sided policy, and it does not report abstain precision on answerable questions.

UAEval4RAG is the closest on metrics: unanswered ratio and acceptable ratio are the right two numbers [28]. But its queries are synthesised per knowledge base, its sweep is over pipeline components rather than thresholds, and it does not fit a threshold on dev and test it on held-out.

The two-axes paper is the closest on policy: two scores, two thresholds, certified budgets [40]. But the answerability score is a hidden-state probe, not a retrieval feature, and the abstract describes no dev/held-out threshold-fitting protocol.

The retrieval-triggering line runs the dependency the other way — retrieve only when the model reports itself unsure — which is a retrieval-budget policy rather than an abstention gate, and in any case keys on model confidence rather than retrieval evidence.

So the gap is real but narrow: retrieval-feature gating with dev-fitted two-sided thresholds and held-out validation of both directions does not appear to exist as a clean published result. It is a legitimate workshop contribution *if* the hardness ladder from 3.2 is built in, because otherwise the result will be another 98%-on-random-negatives paper.

### 3.5 Where the judge's error swamps the effect

AbstentionBench scores abstention with a "quality-verified" LLM judge across 20 datasets and 20 models [27]. The headline effect it reports — a 24% average drop from reasoning fine-tuning — is far larger than plausible judge error, so it survives. The smaller claims in the same paper (a system prompt "boosts abstention") are of the size that a few points of judge noise could manufacture. No paper in this batch reports judge-versus-human agreement on its own abstention labels, which is the number a reader needs to calibrate every other number.

The hallucination benchmarks split into two regimes. Where labels are human and word-level — RAGTruth's 17,790 responses [34] — the judge is not the bottleneck; the bottleneck is that most downstream papers collapse span labels into a binary response label, which changes the base rate and makes AUROC incomparable across papers. Where the judge is a model, the error is documented and large: MT-Bench-era work found judges match human agreement rates while carrying position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases [37], and a later audit quantified twelve bias types [38].

The arithmetic that should worry a reviewer: if a judge mislabels 5% of items at random, a claimed 3-point AUROC gain attenuates toward zero — annoying but safe. If the mislabelling correlates with the feature the method uses — longer answers judged more harshly, hedged answers read as abstentions — the gain is manufactured rather than attenuated. Verbosity bias and hedged-answer confusion are exactly the failure modes that correlate with abstention features. FActScore is the model to copy: it validated its automated scorer against human labels and reported the gap, under 2% [35]. Almost nobody else does.

### 3.6 Cost asymmetry

Every serious decision-theoretic treatment says the abstain/answer choice is a utility comparison, not a threshold on confidence. Learning to defer formalises exactly this: the model may hand an instance to an expert, the loss penalises deferral, and a consistent estimator exists for the joint system [41]. Risk-controlling prediction sets allow the loss to be any bounded, cost-weighted function, with finite-sample control [6], and conformal language modelling lets the user choose the acceptable-answer criterion and the rejection rule [7]. The machinery for a defensible cost model exists and is fifteen years old in spirit.

No benchmark in this area ships one. AbstentionBench's abstention recall treats a refused chemistry question and a refused medical question as equally costly [27]. XSTest and OR-Bench implicitly assert that a false refusal costs the same everywhere [29][30]. SQuAD 2.0's exact-match metric is indifferent to whether the system abstained or answered wrongly. The consequence is that the benchmarks cannot distinguish a gate that refuses the 10% most expensive errors from one that refuses 10% at random — which is the only distinction that matters if you are deploying the thing.

Two rows deserve a warning rather than a number. AbstentionBench is the best-designed abstention benchmark in the batch and the *least* CPU-runnable, because its scoring is judge-based; the honest use on this hardware is to take its per-dataset items and score abstention with a fixed string/heuristic classifier, accepting that your numbers are then not comparable to its published table. RAGTruth's value is its human labels; if you generate your own responses you lose the reason to use it.

On hardness, one line each. SQuAD 2.0 is adversarial by construction but keys on the same paragraph, so its difficulty is largely lexical and it is the wrong benchmark for a retrieval-evidence gate. PopQA has no unanswerable split at all; any unanswerable set you build on top of it is synthetic by definition. TruthfulQA is genuinely hard but measures false-belief imitation, not answerability. XSTest was hard for 2023 models and is now close to saturated. OR-Bench is synthetic by rewriting, so template artefacts are a live risk. HaluEval's negatives are model-generated and therefore style-detectable. RAGTruth is the only row whose hallucination labels are natural and human, which is why it is the one to trust and the one that is hardest to extend. FActScore measures support of atomic claims against a corpus rather than answerability. AbstentionBench's hardest rungs are false premise and underspecification, and its easiest is missing-entity — exactly the ordering a hardness ladder should exploit. UAEval4RAG's negatives are synthesised per knowledge base, which is convenient and unrepresentative. 2WikiMultihopQA ships no unanswerable split and confounds multi-hop retrieval failure with unanswerability.

## 5. Baselines runnable on this hardware

Usable small models, in rough order of how much calibration behaviour I would trust: Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B-Instruct and Llama-3.2-1B/3B-Instruct (llama.cpp or ONNX; my estimate is 10–25 tok/s at Q4 on this CPU, not a measured figure), Gemma-2-2B-it, SmolLM2-1.7B-Instruct. All are RLHF/DPO-tuned, which per the elicitation literature is the regime where verbalised confidence is weakest [15] — so a plan that depends on asking the model "are you sure" should be treated as the null hypothesis, not the method.

The cheap, credible baseline is an ensemble of retrieval and lexical features with a logistic head, not a bigger model:

- BM25 (rank_bm25, pure Python) over the passage set.
- One small dense retriever — bge-small-en-v1.5 (33M) or all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (22M) — on CPU, a few ms per query.
- A cross-encoder reranker, either ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2 (22M) or bge-reranker-base (278M); the 278M one is ~10× slower and rarely changes the ranking enough to matter at this scale.
- Features: top-1 dense cosine, mean of top-5, max BM25, rerank score of top-1, agreement between BM25 top-1 and dense top-1, answer-token log-probability and entropy, and an NLI entailment score of the generated answer against the top passage.
- A logistic regression on ~8 features, thresholds fitted on a dev split, evaluated on held-out.

This is legitimate and it is what the strongest published baselines effectively are: the domain-shift QA calibrator is a trained classifier over model behaviour [5], the post-hoc confidence paper shows a one-line logit normalisation recovers most of the achievable selective performance [4], and the black-box/white-box comparison found the gap between elaborate and simple elicitation to be narrow [16].

What we must match to be taken seriously: 56% coverage at 80% accuracy on a domain-shifted QA mix (the published calibrator number) [5]; non-collapse on high-overlap negatives (the 98%→1% failure must not appear in our table) [31]; and two-sided reporting in the UAEval4RAG style [28]. If our gate cannot beat a single-threshold max-logit baseline at equal abstain precision, that is the result, and it is publishable as a negative result because the post-hoc literature predicts exactly that [4].

## 6. Five candidate gaps

**G1. Two-sided retrieval-feature gate with a hardness ladder.** Fit separate admit/refuse thresholds on retrieval features on dev, validate on held-out, and report abstain precision and recall at each rung of negative hardness (random passage → same-topic near-miss → premise violation). Cheapest experiment: 2,000 PopQA questions, 2,000 SQuAD 2.0 answerables, and four constructed negative rungs, one 1.5B model, one afternoon. Most likely confound: rung hardness correlates with passage length and entity type, so the gate may be learning length rather than answerability — control by matching length distributions across rungs.

**G2. Calibration drift of the gate under retrieval shift.** Same gate, corpus and k varied (k=1,3,5,10; reranker on/off; clean vs. contaminated index), plotting risk–coverage per retrieval condition and reporting ECE of the gate itself. Cheapest experiment: reuse G1's features, add three retrieval conditions, no new labels. Confound: changing k changes both the feature distribution *and* the answerability base rate, so the drift is confounded with a prior shift; fix by re-weighting to a constant base rate.

**G3. Does abstain recall survive when the answerable questions are hard?** Most gates are evaluated on answerable questions that are easy, so "abstain recall on answerable" looks fine while the gate is actually keyed to question difficulty. Stratify answerables by entity popularity (PopQA quintiles) and by retrieval rank of the gold passage. Cheapest experiment: the same 2k PopQA items already carry popularity strata. Confound: popularity correlates with retrieval quality, so a drop may be a retrieval effect rather than a gate effect — control by holding retrieval recall constant across strata.

**G4. A stated cost model, and whether the best gate changes.** Adopt an explicit utility (cost of a wrong answer = 10, cost of a refusal = 1, wrong answer on a high-stakes subset = 50), then ask whether the gate that maximises utility differs from the gate that maximises abstain precision. Cheapest experiment: re-rank existing gates under three cost settings; no new inference. Confound: the cost numbers are invented, so the result is only as meaningful as the sensitivity analysis — report the break-even cost ratio at which the ranking flips, which is defensible without claiming the numbers are real.

**G5. Judge-free abstention scoring.** Replace the LLM judge with a deterministic classifier (refusal-phrase detection plus an entailment check) and measure how much the ranking of gates changes versus a judged score on the same outputs. Cheapest experiment: 1,000 outputs, two scoring functions, rank correlation. Confound: the deterministic scorer has its own bias against hedged partial answers, which is precisely the category abstention research cares about — so the result must be framed as "how much does the judge matter", not "the judge is wrong".

Each of these is small enough for one student with one laptop, and each is a measurement rather than a method — which is the right shape for a workshop paper whose contribution is honesty about what the numbers mean.

# Indic and low-resource NLP — deep dive (S2-6)

## 0. Scope and method

This deep dive asks a narrow question: for a project that must run on one Windows laptop with no CUDA, what about Indic NLP is *measurably broken*, what has already been measured, and what is asserted without measurement? I treated "low-resource" throughout as **compute-poor evaluation**, not small data. The corpora here are not small — IndicCorp v2 alone is 20.9B tokens over 24 languages [2] — so the binding constraint is the evaluator, not the corpus. Any contribution must therefore be a measurement protocol or a probe, not a model.

Discovery ran against Crossref and the arXiv API through the project verifier; OpenAlex was intermittently rate-limited on the shared IP and was used only when it responded. I ran roughly 45 bibliographic queries in five families: Indic corpus/benchmark names, tokenisation and fertility, code-mixing and transliteration, translated-benchmark validity, and abstention/calibration/cross-lingual retrieval. From those I screened several hundred candidate records (about 250 after deduplication) and kept 47 that resolved with a title-similarity of at least 0.90 against API truth. Every reference below is one of those 47. I read abstracts for all of them and full text for nine — the two tokenisation studies [12, 23], the grapheme-tokenizer study [26], MILU [4], Global MMLU [18], IndicQA [7], SemEval-2020 Task 9 [29], IndicGenBench [5] and Aksharantar [16] — plus dataset cards or dataset-server metadata for every row of the table in §4. For the abstention cluster I have abstracts, and for two 2026 preprints [44, 45] only titles and metadata; I flag that in the text wherever it matters.

Two negative results are worth stating up front because they shape §3. First, I found **no** benchmark that measures abstention or calibration in Hindi or Hinglish and none in any Indic language. Second, among the ten datasets in §4 I found **no** dataset containing designed unanswerable or negative items. Both are checkable claims, not impressions: the dataset cards and the papers' construction sections contain no negative-item design, and no query formulation surfaced an Indic abstention probe.

## 1. Taxonomy

**Cluster A — corpora and pretraining resources.** IndicNLPSuite [1] established the pattern that still dominates: release a monolingual corpus, a handful of NLU tasks, and a BERT, in one paper. Its successor [2] is the strongest single resource in the field — IndicCorp v2 at 20.9B tokens across 24 languages from four families, a 2.3× increase over [1] plus twelve new languages, with IndicXTREME as the accompanying benchmark (nine tasks, 20 languages, 105 evaluation sets of which 52 were new) and IndicBERT v2 improving by an absolute 2 points averaged over languages and tasks. Samanantar [3] added 49.7M English–Indic sentence pairs over 11 languages, of which 37.4M were mined from the web — a 4× increase — and remains the parallel workhorse. Aksharantar [16] covers the other half of Indian text reality: 26M transliteration pairs over 21 languages and 12 scripts, 21× larger than what existed, plus a 103k-pair test set split by word frequency and named-entity origin. Bhasa-Abhijnaanam [17] supplies language identification for 22 languages in both native and romanised script, which is the component every Hinglish pipeline silently assumes.

**Cluster B — evaluation benchmarks.** Three design philosophies coexist. *Extend-and-translate*: IndicGenBench [5] takes CrossSum, XQuAD, XorQA and FLORES and extends them by human curation to 29 Indic languages across 13 scripts and 5 tasks, with test sets of roughly 14.3k–29.3k items per task; IndicQA [7] assembles 11 languages from translated XQuAD, MLQA, NQ-Open, MS MARCO and LlamaIndex, a Hindi SQuAD translated with the Google Translate API, and a Gemini-generated synthetic set with manual verification. *Collect-native*: TyDi QA [10] is the reference example of questions written by speakers of the language rather than translated into it; Belebele [9] is 900 human-written multiple-choice questions per language variant over 122 variants, built on 488 FLORES passages, fully parallel so cross-language comparisons are legitimate. *Exam-harvest*: MILU [4] takes 79,617 multiple-choice items from Indian regional and state examinations across 8 domains, 41 subjects and 11 languages, and reports GPT-4o at 74% average with open multilingual models beating Indic-specific fine-tunes. MGSM [41] is the translated-benchmark archetype for reasoning — the same grade-school math problems across ten typologically diverse languages — and it is the place where translation effects on chain-of-thought are easiest to see. IndicNLG [6], IndicMMLU-Pro [19] and IndicParam [24] are variations on the same three moves.

**Cluster C — tokenisation and script.** This is the cluster with the hardest numbers. Rust et al. [13] showed that tokenizer quality predicts monolingual performance of multilingual models independently of model size; Ahia et al. [12] converted that into money, finding close to a 5× cost increase for mid-resourced Indic languages in non-Latin scripts relative to English on commercial APIs, and up to 4× on prompt-plus-generation for Telugu and Amharic on XLSum; Petrov et al. [25] measured across 17 tokenizers and found token-length differences up to 15× between language pairs, still above 4× for character- and byte-level models. The 2024–2026 work moves from complaint to repair: [14] accelerates inference for excessively tokenised languages, [23] is a systematic study over 17 Indic languages and 11 scripts covering BPE versus Unigram, script-aware normalisation, vocabulary size and vocabulary construction, [26] evaluates grapheme-cluster tokenizers on 11 Indic languages with fertility, Rényi efficiency and percentile frequency as intrinsic measures, and [27] and [28] attack vocabulary design and byte-level chunking for cross-lingual transfer. [35] is an earlier, smaller BERT-tokenizer study on an Indian language.

**Cluster D — code-mixing and transliteration.** GLUECoS [15] defined the task suite for Hindi-English and Spanish-English; SemEval-2020 Task 9 [29] released 20k Hinglish and 19k Spanglish tweets with word-level language tags and three-way sentiment; L3Cube-HingCorpus [43] scaled the corpus side; SentiGrad [32] and SentMix-3L [34] are recent additions. The consistent finding across this cluster is that gains are small and unstable, which §3 explains.

**Cluster E — evaluation validity.** Global MMLU [18] is the key paper: 42 languages assembled from 14 professional translations with post-edits, 11 crowdsourced translations and 16 machine translations, with 200 annotators labelling 2,850 MMLU items. They find 28% of sampled questions require Western-centric knowledge and 84.9% of geographic references point at North America or Europe; they also find that model rankings move by an average of 3.7 positions on the culturally-agnostic subset and 7.3 on the culturally-sensitive subset relative to a uniform English MMLU subsample, and that human-translated and machine-translated versions diverge most in low-resource languages. INCLUDE [40] takes the opposite route — 197,243 QA pairs drawn from local exam sources across 44 written languages, Apache-2.0 — and is the cleanest available control for the translation confound.

**Cluster F — retrieval, uncertainty and trust.** Multilingual RAG for knowledge-intensive QA [20] is the direct Indic-relevant retrieval paper I could verify; [46] reports a "multilingual paradox" in embedding models for Turkish, where retrieval quality in-language is worse than through translation. On the uncertainty side, [21], [22], [30] and [31] analyse abstention failures, recognition-refusal misalignment, attribution-versus-abstention, and latent skill selection under contextual uncertainty — all English. XTRUST [36] evaluates multilingual trustworthiness dimensions but I only have its title and abstract, so I do not treat it as an abstention measurement.

## 2. What is saturated

Four moves are exhausted. **Another translated MMLU.** IndicMMLU-Pro [19] and IndicParam [24] join MILU [4] in the same design space; after Global MMLU [18] demonstrated that translation provenance moves rankings by 3.7–7.3 positions, a new translated leaderboard adds a row, not knowledge. **Another monolingual Indic encoder.** IndicBERT v2 [2] and MuRIL [37] already cover the bases; a third BERT trained on the same IndicCorp is an engineering exercise. **Another code-mixed sentiment SOTA.** SemEval-9 [29] has an enormous system-paper literature and a hard plateau; [32] and [34] report new datasets without resolving the annotation-noise problem that §3 describes. **Restating the tokenizer penalty.** The 5× and 15× numbers [12, 25] are established; a paper that re-measures them on one more tokenizer contributes a footnote.

What is *not* saturated is measurement: nobody reports the within-benchmark difference between native and translated items, nobody reports abstention rates in an Indic language, and nobody reports whether the tokenizer penalty changes model *rankings* rather than just cost.

## 3. What is genuinely open

### 3.1 The translation confound sits inside MILU, unanalysed

MILU publishes per-language counts of translated versus native items, and the distribution is extreme. Of 79,617 test items, 19,915 are translations. But the share is wildly uneven by language: Hindi has 14,837 items of which only 115 are translated (under 1%); Punjabi has 4,099 of which 3,411 are translated (83%); Malayalam 3,354 of 4,321 (78%); Odia 3,100 of 4,525 (69%); Gujarati 2,755 of 4,827 (57%). MILU's headline finding — models do better in high-resource than low-resource languages — is therefore confounded with "the low-resource languages' items are mostly translations from English". Both explanations predict the same curve.

Nobody has reported the native-versus-translated gap *within* a language, holding the benchmark fixed. This is the single most exploitable observation in this review, because the metadata already exists and the experiment costs one CPU afternoon. The confound is that translated and native items are not randomly assigned: translation was applied where native items were unavailable, which correlates with subject and difficulty, so a naive comparison measures item mix as much as translation.

### 3.2 Abstention and calibration in Hindi are not measured anywhere

Stated plainly: I found no dataset, benchmark or paper that measures whether a model knows it does not know in Hindi, Hinglish, or any Indic language. There is no Indic counterpart to the English abstention literature [21, 22, 30, 31]. MILU, IndicMMLU-Pro, IndicParam, Belebele and IndicQA are all answerable by construction — every item has exactly one correct option or a gold span in the context, with no abstain option and no deliberately unanswerable items. Belebele is four-way multiple choice with exactly one correct answer [9]; FLORES contains no questions at all, only parallel sentences [11].

The closest adjacent work is three things, none of them the measurement. First, English abstention analyses: [21] on abstention failures in reasoning models, [22] on why models answer structurally unanswerable questions, [30] on attribution-versus-abstention in long-document QA, [31] on latent skill selection under contextual uncertainty. Second, [45] — a 2026 Research Square preprint by Speranza, pre-registered, comparing four abstention *interfaces* in a small language model with versioned memory. It is methodologically the nearest neighbour to a probe design, and it is English-only; I have its record and abstract but not its full text. Third, [44] — a 2026 SSRN preprint arguing that heuristic refusal classifiers systematically undercount refusals by reasoning models in Indic languages. That is a measurement-instrument critique, not a calibration study, and I have only its title and metadata; but it is decisive for design, because it says any Hindi abstention metric will be corrupted by the fact that models refuse in English while being prompted in Hindi.

That corruption is the central design problem, and it is why this is a real gap rather than an oversight. A refusal detector trained on English strings will miscount a Hindi refusal as an answer; a human annotator will not. So the probe must fix the *language of the refusal* as a measured variable, not a preprocessing detail.

### 3.3 The tokenizer penalty is measured; its effect on rankings is not

The magnitudes are settled: up to 15× across languages and over 4× even at byte level [25]; close to 5× API cost for mid-resourced Indic languages in non-Latin scripts [12]; a documented consequence that Telugu cannot fit even one in-context demonstration inside ChatGPT's 4,096-token window on XLSum [12]. What is *not* settled is whether this changes conclusions. Two controls give different answers and nobody runs both: match on **token budget** (give Hindi and English the same number of tokens — Hindi sees less content, so Hindi scores drop and the drop is an artefact of tokenisation) or match on **information** (give Hindi 5× the tokens — cost rises, scores may equalise). A paper that reports only one control cannot separate "the model is worse at Hindi" from "we fed it less Hindi".

The fertility numbers themselves are also more design-dependent than the field admits. [23] finds Hindi fertility of 1.73 tokens per word under a tuned grapheme-pair tokenizer but 3.64 under a heavily vocabulary-constrained variant — a 2.1× swing from vocabulary choice alone. So "the" Hindi multiplier is not a property of Hindi; it is a property of a tokenizer configuration, and any claim should name the tokenizer and the vocabulary size.

### 3.4 Hinglish evaluation has an annotation ceiling nobody reports

SemEval-2020 Task 9 [29] released 20k Hinglish tweets with three sentiment classes and reports an **inter-annotator agreement of 55%**, with two annotators per item and discordant items discarded. The best system in the shared task reached 75.0 F1. A model scoring 75 F1 against labels that humans agree on 55% of the time is not measuring sentiment; it is partly fitting annotator idiosyncrasy. The average code-mixing index was about 25, meaning most "code-mixed" tweets are only mildly mixed.

Newer datasets in this space — SentiGrad [32], SentMix-3L [34], L3Cube-HingCorpus [43] — do not report IAA in their abstracts. So the field routinely claims 1–3 point improvements on a target whose noise floor is larger than the claimed effect. This is a cheap, honest paper: re-annotate a few hundred items, publish the ceiling, and show which reported gains survive it.

### 3.5 Licensing blocks most artefact reproduction

This is not discussed in any paper I read, and it is checkable. XL-Sum is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 — non-commercial *and* share-alike [8]. Samanantar is CC BY-NC 4.0 [3]. Both are the default Hindi training and evaluation data for summarisation and translation, and neither can be redistributed inside a permissively licensed artefact. FLORES-200 is CC BY-SA 4.0 and gated [11]; Belebele is CC BY-SA 4.0 [9]; IndicGenBench is CC BY-SA 4.0 [5]; MILU is CC BY 4.0 but gated with automatic approval [4]; INCLUDE is Apache-2.0 [40]; IndicCorp v2's card states CC-0 in prose while the API licence field is empty [2] — the one genuinely clean large corpus, with a metadata inconsistency worth quoting. The practical consequence: a paper that bundles Hindi data can be at best CC BY-SA, and must exclude XL-Sum and Samanantar entirely, or it is redistributing non-commercial material.

### 3.6 Which benchmarks can carry a claim

At n=500 the 95% confidence half-width on an accuracy near 60% is ±4.3 percentage points; at n=550 it is ±4.1; at n=900, ±3.2; at n=1,000, ±3.0. Most head-to-head Indic comparisons in the literature are reported on per-language samples in the 500–1,000 range, which means a 3-point gap is inside the noise. Sizes that matter: Belebele gives 900 per variant [9]; MILU's smallest language, Punjabi, has 4,099 items (±1.5pp) [4]; IndicGenBench's ~14.5k test items spread over 29 languages give roughly 500 each [5]; INCLUDE's released split has about 550 test and 25 validation rows per language, so ±4.1pp [40]; FLORES devtest has 1,012 sentences [11], fine for corpus BLEU but the segment count is what drives variance. Anything reporting per-language results on 200–300 items — several older Indic NLU task sets fall here — cannot support a comparative claim at all.

## 5. Baselines runnable on this hardware

Parameter counts below are from the model files (safetensors totals include embeddings), not from model names.

**Embedding models.** `multilingual-e5-small` is 117.7M parameters under MIT [47] — the correct default for this laptop: 384 dimensions, 512-token limit, and cheap enough on CPU that encoding a few thousand sentences is a coffee break rather than a project. `multilingual-e5-base` is 278.0M, also MIT, and is the quality step up if you can afford roughly 2.5× the time. LaBSE (470.9M parameters, Apache-2.0, per its model card) is the strongest off-the-shelf bitext miner and the most expensive of the three; its 512-token limit and 471M parameters make it a batch tool, not an interactive one. `Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B` (595.8M, Apache-2.0) supports long context and scores well multilingually, but on this CPU it is an order of magnitude slower than e5-small — use it only for a final retrieval table, not for iteration.

**Encoders for classification.** MuRIL base [37] is 12 layers × 768 hidden with a 197,285-token vocabulary, Apache-2.0, and was pretrained on both native script and romanised Indic text — the one model where Hinglish input is not out-of-distribution. IndicBERT v2 [2] is 12 × 768 with a 250,000-token vocabulary, MIT, trained on IndicCorp v2 across 24 languages. Both fine-tune on CPU for a small classification head in minutes-to-an-hour at a few thousand examples; neither generates.

**Small generative models.** Qwen3-0.6B (751.6M parameters including embeddings) and Qwen3-1.7B (2.03B) are Apache-2.0 [38] and are the practical range for this laptop; 1.7B in 4-bit GGUF is workable for batch scoring, and 0.6B is the sane choice for thousand-item sweeps. Gemma 3 4B (4.30B parameters) [39] is a stronger model under a custom Gemma licence, not Apache-2.0, with redistribution and use restrictions — fine for experiments, awkward for an artefact. At 4B on a CPU without CUDA, plan for offline batch only, not interactive use. For translate-test pivots, IndicTrans2 [42] provides 22-language Indic translation with distilled variants that fit the hardware, and the WMT 2024 MultiIndic22MT findings [33] give an honest picture of where Indic MT still fails.

Two cost rules I would enforce in the experiment plan. First, measure your own throughput once with a fixed prompt before designing anything — vendor and blog numbers are not transferable to an Arc iGPU. Second, budget in *prefill tokens*, not items: MILU Hindi is 14,837 items × 4 options × ~220 tokens ≈ 13.1M prefill tokens, which is 36 hours at 100 tokens/s and 90 hours at 40. A 1,000-item stratified subsample costs 2.4 hours at 100 tokens/s and buys ±3.0pp, which is better science per hour than a full sweep with one run per item.

## 6. Five candidate gaps

**G1 — The native-versus-translated item gap inside MILU.** MILU ships a per-item translation flag and a per-language translation share that ranges from under 1% to 83% [4]. Nobody has reported accuracy split by provenance within a language. Cheapest experiment: sample 1,000 items from Hindi (almost all native) and 1,000 from Punjabi (83% translated), stratify by subject, score with Qwen3-1.7B and one API model, and report the native-versus-translated delta with confidence intervals. Confound: translation was applied where native items were scarce, so provenance correlates with subject and difficulty; stratification fixes the subject mix but not the unmeasured difficulty difference, and a null result would be uninterpretable without a difficulty-matched control.

**G2 — A Hindi/Hinglish abstention and calibration probe.** No such measurement exists (§3.2). Cheapest experiment: take 400 answerable Hindi items from Belebele or MILU, construct 200 unanswerable counterparts by pairing each question with a passage that does not support it (or by asking for an entity absent from the context), and measure refusal rate, over-refusal on the answerable controls, and expected calibration error on the option logits. Score refusals with a language-aware rule plus 100 human-labelled items, and report agreement between the two. Confound: "unanswerable from context" is not "unanswerable" — a model may answer correctly from parametric memory, which converts a good refusal into a false answer and makes the model look worse for knowing more. The control is a second condition using entities invented for the experiment.

**G3 — Token budget versus information budget in few-shot Indic evaluation.** Give Hindi and English the same token budget, and separately the same information content (5× the Hindi tokens), on 300 Belebele items with Qwen3-0.6B. If model rankings flip between the two controls, the field's cross-lingual comparisons are measuring tokenisers. Confound: the two conditions differ in prompt length as well as content, so length effects and tokenisation effects are entangled unless you add a length-matched English condition with padding.

**G4 — The Hinglish annotation ceiling.** Re-annotate 300 SemEval-9 Hinglish items with three annotators, compute pairwise agreement and the accuracy ceiling implied by the labels [29], then re-score two published models against the new labels. Confound: a new annotator pool differs in dialect exposure and Hindi proficiency from the 2020 pool, so any disagreement is not purely "noise" but a population difference — which is itself the interesting result, but it weakens the ceiling claim.

**G5 — Chunking under fertility in cross-lingual RAG.** The 5× token penalty [12] means a fixed-token chunk holds roughly one-fifth the Devanagari content of an English chunk, and [20] and [46] suggest retrieval degrades when the query and the corpus differ in language or when the embedding model is not tuned for the target language. Cheapest experiment: 200 Hindi questions over a small Hindi Wikipedia dump, comparing chunk-by-tokens versus chunk-by-characters with e5-small and Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, measuring recall@5 against gold passages. Confound: gold passage selection depends on the chunking, so chunking and ground truth are not independent — build the gold set at document level, not chunk level, or the comparison is circular.

Of these, G2 is the one that is both genuinely unmeasured and cheap; G1 is the cheapest and most likely to produce a clean, quotable number; G3 is the most likely to be scooped by a tokenisation paper, since that literature is moving fast [23, 26, 27, 28].

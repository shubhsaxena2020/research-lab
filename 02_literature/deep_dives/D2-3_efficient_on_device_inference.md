# Efficient on-device inference and small models — deep dive (S2-4)

## 0. Scope and method

This dive covers the deployment half of the efficiency literature: how a fixed model is made to fit and run on hardware that has no CUDA device, and how that claim is measured. The searches were run through the project's citation tool against arXiv and OpenAlex with fourteen query families: post-training quantisation by method name (GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant, LLM.int8), GGUF/k-quant format comparisons, KV-cache eviction and paging, speculative decoding including CPU-specific variants, small-model technical reports (Qwen3, Llama 3, Gemma 3, Phi-4-mini, SmolLM2, TinyLlama), edge/NPU inference benchmarks, and — most usefully — reproducibility and measurement studies ("inference backends reproducibility", "energy per token", "measurement study trade-offs edge continuum"). Roughly 140 records were screened at title and abstract level, 40 were carried into verification (36 in the first pass plus four re-resolutions after title mismatches), and 32 appear here. Ten were read at results level, not just abstract: the llama.cpp quantisation sweep, the speculative-decoding anatomy paper, the backend-reproducibility study, the edge-continuum measurement study, the mobile/NPU benchmark, the deployment-aware reasoning evaluation, the small-language-model survey with runtime measurements, plus GPTQ, H2O and KIVI for their headline numbers.

Two caveats on the corpus. First, the literature here is bimodal: method papers from 2022–2024 that are almost entirely GPU-centric (A100, H100, or "desktop and mobile GPUs" in the vendor sense), and a 2025–2026 layer of measurement papers that are much more honest and much more relevant to this project. Second, the small-model technical reports are marketing documents as much as papers; their numbers are real, but the suite is chosen by the model's owner.

## 1. Taxonomy

### 1.1 Weight-only post-training quantisation: four methods, one idea

GPTQ quantises layers one at a time with approximate second-order information, correcting the error introduced in earlier columns as it goes [1]: 175B parameters to 3–4 bits in about four GPU hours, with end-to-end speedups of 3.25× on an A100 and 4.5× on an A6000 that come from halving bytes moved. AWQ observes that salient channels can be identified from the *activation* distribution rather than the weights, and that protecting roughly 1% of them by an equivalent per-channel rescale recovers most of the lost quality while keeping a uniform 4-bit kernel; its TinyChat runtime claims more than 3× over HuggingFace FP16 on desktop and mobile GPUs [2]. SmoothQuant and LLM.int8 attack the other operand: LLM.int8 isolates the few systematic outlier dimensions whose magnitude grows with model scale into a 16-bit matmul while 99.9% of values stay 8-bit, halving memory at full precision [4]; SmoothQuant migrates quantisation difficulty from activations to weights offline, enabling W8A8 up to 530B with 1.56× speedup and 2× memory reduction [3]. Both are elegant and both are largely inapplicable here: activation quantisation buys tensor-core throughput, and llama.cpp computes activations in fp16/fp32 and compresses only weights, so the W8A8 literature explains a speedup a CPU cannot collect.

### 1.2 GGUF and k-quants: the format that actually ships on a laptop

The format-level literature is thinner but far more decision-relevant. One 2026 study fixes a single model (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), one toolchain (llama.cpp b7600), one harness (lm_eval 0.4.9.2) and one machine (dual Xeon 8488C, 192 threads), and sweeps thirteen GGUF formats from Q3_K_S to Q8_0 [5]. Three findings matter. The cliff is not where folklore puts it: Q3_K_S loses 9.32 points on GSM8K (77.63 → 68.31) and 5.73% on the five-benchmark mean, while Q4_K_S and Q4_K_M are indistinguishable from F16 (77.33, 77.41 versus 77.63). Format identity matters as much as nominal width: Q5_K_S scores 75.66 on GSM8K while Q5_0, at the same nominal width, scores 79.08. And three different 5-bit formats share an identical WikiText-2 perplexity of 7.43 yet differ by 3.4 points on GSM8K and 1.5 on IFEval — perplexity is not a sufficient statistic for what users care about. The same paper supplies the only clean CPU series I found for a fixed model across formats: token generation goes 2.83 tok/s at F16, 4.65 at Q4_K_S, 9.91 at Q3_K_S, while prefill moves non-monotonically (79.57, 97.35 for Q4_0, 55.20 for Q4_1) — pp and tg have different bottlenecks and must be reported separately.

### 1.3 KV-cache compression and paging

PagedAttention stores the KV cache in fixed-size blocks with a block table, so sequences need no contiguous reservation and blocks can be shared copy-on-write; the measured effect is 2–4× serving throughput at equal latency against FasterTransformer and Orca, growing with sequence length [9]. That is a batching story and does nothing for a single-user laptop. Eviction and compression are the single-stream story. H2O keeps recent tokens plus a small set of attention "heavy hitters" under a fixed budget, reporting up to 29× throughput at a 20% budget and 1.9× lower latency at equal batch [10]. SnapKV predicts each head's attention pattern from an observation window at the end of the prompt and selects clustered positions per head: 3.6× generation speed and 8.2× memory efficiency at 16K input across 16 long-sequence datasets [11]. KIVI quantises instead of dropping, and makes the structural point that keys need per-channel and values per-token quantisation; at 2 bits it reports 2.6× lower peak memory including weights and 2.35–3.47× throughput [12]. The most useful paper in the cluster benchmarks the others: a workload-aware comparison of KIVI, TurboQuant, SnapKV and CaM on Llama-3.1-8B and Mistral-7B across four LongBench-style workload families, measuring quality, throughput, TTFT and *realised* compression ratio [13]. Its conclusion is the one to internalise — nominal compression ratio poorly predicts end-to-end behaviour, KIVI-4bit is the most stable across models, SnapKV gives the best long-context throughput, and merging methods are wildly workload-sensitive.

### 1.4 Speculative and draft-model decoding

Leviathan et al. show that a small approximation model can propose tokens which the large model verifies in one batched pass, with a sampling rule that provably preserves the target distribution: 2–3× on T5-XXL with identical outputs [14]. Chen et al. do the same for Chinchilla 70B, reporting 2–2.5× [15]. Both buy latency with extra compute — a trade a GPU likes and a bandwidth-bound CPU does not obviously like. The paper that should anchor any CPU claim is a from-scratch implementation across five draft/target/backend configurations on a consumer laptop [16]. Its best configuration (0.5B q8_0 draft, 3B fp16 target) reaches 1.61× at K=6 with acceptance falling from 69.7% at K=1 to 37.8% at the optimum — but **three of five configurations decelerate**, and the paper isolates why: at batch sizes 2–7 the quantised Metal backend runs verification as per-position matrix-vector products, so a 7-token verify costs 218.5 ms against 51.0 ms for a 2-token pass, flattening to ~141 ms only above batch 9 where the kernel switches to matrix-matrix. A torch-MPS verifier extends its cache with 5 tokens in 42.3 ms versus 35.2 ms for one (1.20×) and is genuinely parallel. Speculative decoding pays only when verification is actually batch-parallel, and a small-batch CPU GGUF backend is the serial case by construction.

### 1.5 Distillation and the small-model families that exist today

Qwen3 spans 0.6B to 235B, dense and MoE, Apache-2.0, and states that its small models are trained with knowledge from the flagship models — strong-to-weak distillation — alongside a thinking/non-thinking mode switch and a thinking budget [18]. Gemma 3 (270M/1B/4B/12B/27B) is trained with distillation and claims 4B-IT is competitive with Gemma 2's 27B-IT; its architecture also raises the local-to-global attention ratio specifically to cut long-context KV memory [20]. Llama 3.2 added 1B and 3B tiers [19]; Phi-4-mini is a 3.8B model assembled by mixture-of-LoRAs [21]; SmolLM2 is 1.7B trained on ~11T curated tokens and claims to beat Qwen2.5-1.5B and Llama-3.2-1B [22]; TinyLlama is the older 1.1B/3T-token baseline most of this generation is measured against [23].

The 57-model survey is the best source on what these cost at runtime, because it benchmarks 20 of them under one harness — llama.cpp, 2048-token context, one Jetson Orin NX (GPU) and one smartphone (CPU) — measuring prefill, decode, memory and energy [24]. Memory across 0.1B–3B spans 275 MB to 2456 MB, a 9× range for a 30× parameter range, because vocabulary and KV allocation dominate at the small end (Gemma's 256k vocabulary and Bloom's 250k are singled out). Architecture also beats parameter count at the margin: Qwen1.5-0.5B has 25.4% more parameters than Qwen2-0.5B but runs 31.9% faster on the same device, because Qwen2 shares the embedding and LM head and spends its parameters on attention and FFN.

What these reports' tables do **not** imply: "4B beats last generation's 27B" is a claim about one suite, one prompt format, the winner's own tokenizer and chat template — and it says nothing about the 2 GB, batch-1, 8-thread regime this project can run, where the model must be quantised to 4 bits, a perturbation the report never applies to itself. SmolLM2's win over Qwen2.5-1.5B was true of the January-2025 checkpoints on the authors' suite; reported numbers are a snapshot of a moving object.

### 1.6 Runtimes

llama.cpp is the de facto CPU runtime of the measured literature — the engine in [5], [16], [22] and one of five in [28]. MLC/TVM appears as the Android path over Adreno, MLX on Apple silicon, and MLX notably does *not* target the Neural Engine, so "Apple NPU" claims in the wild are usually GPU claims [25]. The Hailo-10H is the one independent edge-NPU measurement: 6.9 tok/s on Qwen2.5-1.5B 4-bit at under 2 W, thermally stable, and bandwidth-limited rather than compute-limited [25]. The five studies closest to this regime use llama.cpp [16,22,28], Ollama [25,27], TVM/MLC on Android and MLX on iOS [25] — and not one uses ONNX Runtime, OpenVINO or DirectML, the three stacks a Windows laptop with an Arc iGPU would actually choose. The laptop-CPU regime has measured baselines but no standard harness.

### 1.7 The measurement literature, where the real content is

The backend-reproducibility study surveys 200 inference engines and 35,000 papers, then holds weights, prompts, decoding parameters and hardware fixed while varying only the engine across five backends including vLLM, SGLang and llama.cpp [28]. The spread reaches **16.6 percentage points** on GSM8K (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B: 78.47% on the transformers reference versus 61.87% on Ollama), no backend matches the reference everywhere, and of 2,257 surveyed papers without code only 36% named their backend at all. The edge-continuum study measures Jetson AGX Orin against a near-edge server in CPU-only and GPU modes over Llama-3.2-1B/3B at F16/Q5_K_M/Q4_K_M, Mistral-7B, TinyLlama and Phi-2 through Ollama, with GPT-4o as an accuracy reference (~80%) [27]: GPU server wins latency, Jetson wins measured energy, CPU-only is dominated on both, and **parameter count and file size do not predict accuracy or latency**. The mobile/NPU study fixes one model (Qwen2.5-1.5B 4-bit), one 258-token prompt and 20 warm iterations across four platforms [25]: the iPhone 16 Pro loses ~40% of peak throughput within three iterations and plateaus near 23.7 tok/s, the S24 Ultra loses ~15% over 20 to a governor-regulated ~10 tok/s, an RTX 4050 sustains 131.7 tok/s at 34.1 W, and the Hailo NPU does 6.9 tok/s under 2 W. Sustained, not peak, is the only number that means anything for an always-on agent.

Two supporting artifacts supply conventions rather than findings. A roofline-based survey gives the analytical frame: decode is memory-bound so tokens/s is bytes-per-token over achievable bandwidth, prefill is compute-bound, and the two must be reported separately — its LLM-Viewer tool makes the arithmetic-intensity calculation mechanical [29]. TokenPowerBench attributes measured power to prefill and decode per request across batch size, context length, parallelism and quantisation, without specialised meters, from 1B to 405B [26]. Both are GPU-first, and both define the convention — separated phases, joules per token, stated configuration — that a CPU study should copy.

## 2. What is saturated

Adding another weight-only PTQ method is saturated: GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant and LLM.int8 already span the design space (second-order correction, activation-aware scaling, outlier decomposition, W8A8 migration), and every subsequent variant I screened reports the same shape of result — a perplexity table on Llama/Qwen/OPT at 3–4 bits with "negligible" degradation and a GPU speedup. Another KV eviction heuristic is likewise saturated: heavy hitters, observation windows, per-channel key quantisation and merging already cover the mechanism space, and the 2026 benchmark shows the differences between them are workload-dependent rather than uniformly ordered [13]; a survey already taxonomises the field into token-, model- and system-level strategies, so a new taxonomy is a fifth wheel [31]. Another "4-bit is nearly lossless" claim is saturated and, worse, usually wrong at the margin: the format sweep finds a real 9-point GSM8K loss at 3-bit and a 3.4-point GSM8K spread between three formats with identical perplexity [5].

Small-model leaderboard papers are saturated in a different way: each new family ships a table where it beats the previous generation on its own suite, and the tables are not comparable across families because prompt format, few-shot count, tokenizer and judge all differ. Survey papers are saturated too — I screened five and cite one only for its taxonomy [31]. What is *not* saturated is anything with a disclosed protocol.

## 3. What is genuinely open

**Is there a credible Pareto measurement of quality vs peak RAM vs latency for CPU-only small models on a fixed task?** Not that I can find, and I looked specifically. The nearest candidates each drop one axis. [24] has capability + latency + memory + energy for 57 models but on Jetson GPU and smartphone CPU, not a laptop CPU, and reports memory as a footprint number rather than a peak-RSS protocol. [27] has accuracy + latency + energy + footprint with a Pareto analysis, but five models, one QA workload, no laptop, and no CPU-vs-CPU comparison of the same model family across quantisation levels. [5] has quality + size + throughput for thirteen formats but one model, one machine, and no memory measurement at all. [30] has accuracy + latency + peak VRAM with confidence intervals, but on GPU and across model families rather than within one. Nobody has published quality-vs-peak-RAM-vs-latency frontiers for 0.3B–4B models, CPU-only, on one fixed task, with repeats and disclosed thread count. The pieces exist; the assembly does not.

**int8/int4 quality deltas on downstream tasks, and whether perplexity predicts them.** Four papers report actual downstream deltas rather than perplexity: [5] on reasoning/knowledge/instruction-following/truthfulness, [6] on code generation across six quantisation methods and two code-model families with McEval and CoderEval, [7] on reasoning models, and [8] which shows that equal accuracy can hide large numbers of answer flips — compressed models change *which* questions they get right, and MT-Bench quality drops even when accuracy is flat. The answer to "does perplexity predict task quality" is a documented no: identical 7.43 perplexity with 3.4 points of GSM8K difference in [5]; and [8]'s flips are invisible to any aggregate. This is the most reusable negative result in the area, and it is under-exploited — most papers still lead with perplexity.

**Is the "2 GB RAM budget" standardised?** No, and the three papers that use a memory number each mean something different. [27] reports "model footprint" (the weight file). [16] reports peak RSS as a process-wide high-water mark and explicitly calls it an upper bound. [24] reports llama.cpp's allocated memory with context fixed at 2048, and notes the trap that llama.cpp allocates KV cache and compute buffer according to the model's *maximum* context unless overridden — so an unmodified run of a 128K-context model can allocate orders of magnitude more than the weights. MLPerf-style suites do not define a laptop RAM budget scenario at all. Anyone claiming "runs in 2 GB" is therefore making one of at least four different claims; a paper that says which one, and reports both file size and peak RSS, would be doing something the field currently does not.

**Does speculative decoding help on CPU when the bottleneck is bandwidth?** The careful answer is: only under conditions that CPU backends usually fail. [16] is the best evidence available and it is mostly negative — three of five configurations decelerate, and the mechanism is that verification is not batch-parallel at small batch sizes on a quantised backend. A CPU decode step is bandwidth-bound, so verifying K+1 positions in one pass should read the weights once and cost barely more than one step; but llama.cpp's CPU kernels are mat-vector at small batch, which reads the weights per position. The crossover observed on Metal was at batch ~9. The one CPU paper that does report a win makes the same point from the opposite direction: SubTernary gets 3.99× over plain autoregressive decoding and 1.35× over a llama.cpp-style speculative baseline, and its own ablation attributes 35.0% of the throughput gain to systems work (single memory arena, persistent thread pool, zero-copy KV rollback via a static scratchpad) against 6.0% for its 256-bit ternary kernel [17]. In other words, on CPU the speculative machinery is an *operating-systems* problem — context-switch latency between draft and target graphs, and dynamic reallocation during rollback — not a numerical one. That is a very different bottleneck from the GPU literature's, and it means the honest prediction for an unmodified laptop CPU stack is: little or no gain from turning on a draft model, large gain only after removing the per-round allocation and thread-synchronisation costs. Nobody has published the vanilla x86 llama.cpp sweep that would settle it, which makes it a genuinely open, cheap experiment.

**Controlled designs where the model family is fixed and only deployment varies.** Four exist and they are the methodological models to copy. [28] fixes weights and hardware and varies only the engine — the cleanest single-variable design in the literature. [16] fixes draft/target pairs and varies backend and K across five configurations. [13] fixes two models and varies the KV mechanism, reporting quality, throughput, TTFT and realised compression together. [5] fixes one model and toolchain and varies only the format. What none of them does is hold the family fixed while varying *quantisation level* and measuring quality, peak RAM and latency jointly with repeats — which is precisely the design a student with one laptop can execute. One adjacent paper shows how much leverage a controlled small-model protocol has even without a runtime axis: nine open models between 135M and 3B on a single 1,085-example, 16-topic multiple-choice benchmark, where Qwen Coder 3B leads base evaluation at 75.67% strict accuracy, and a shared 4-bit NF4 + DoRA/LoRA pipeline then moves that same model by +26.85 points on a 108-example held-out split, SmolLM2-1.7B by +25.92 and SmolLM2-135M by +5.55 [32]. The adaptation delta is larger than most of the between-model gaps — which is a warning for anyone planning to attribute a quality difference to quantisation or to model choice without controlling for fine-tuning.

For a single fixed task under a RAM budget, GSM8K-500-items plus a 200-prompt IFEval subset is the practical core, and IFEval should be sized by its *output* length rather than its prompt count: both are small, both are exact-match verifiable without a judge model, and both are sensitive to quantisation in the directions the literature reports. A 70B judge is not needed and would not fit the hardware; any design that requires one is out of scope for this project and should be said so explicitly.

## 5. Baselines runnable on this hardware

Assumptions, stated because the numbers below are estimates and nothing here measured a Core Ultra 5 225H: decode throughput is bandwidth-bound, so tokens/s ≈ effective_bandwidth ÷ (weight bytes + KV bytes at the stated context). Effective bandwidth for llama.cpp decode is taken as 25–35 GB/s on a dual-channel LPDDR5x laptop (~100 GB/s peak, 30–35% realised), weights at Q4_K_M ≈ 0.60 bytes/param and Q8_0 ≈ 1.06, KV at 2048 tokens in fp16 from the model config, plus ~0.3 GB process overhead. The calibration anchor is [5]'s measurement on a 192-thread Xeon: 4.65 tok/s at Q4_K_S for an 8B model, i.e. ~23 GB/s effective, and 2.83 tok/s at F16 (~45 GB/s effective) — a laptop should land in the same band, not above it. Treat every figure as ±50%.

For measured anchors rather than estimates, three exist and should be quoted instead whenever possible: 4.65 tok/s for an 8B at Q4_K_S on 96 physical cores [5]; 6.9 tok/s for Qwen2.5-1.5B 4-bit on a Hailo-10H at under 2 W, and 23.7 / ~10 tok/s sustained on an iPhone 16 Pro / S24 Ultra [25]; and 16.0–74.0 tok/s for 0.5B+1.5B/3B pairs on Apple silicon under llama.cpp-Metal and torch-MPS [16]. The last is the only published set with peak RSS attached (5.23 GB for the 3B fp16 target plus draft), which is exactly the reporting convention this project should adopt.

Two hardware-specific notes. The Arc iGPU is reachable from llama.cpp (SYCL/Vulkan backends) and from ONNX Runtime/DirectML, and a 4B Q4 model is small enough that iGPU memory is not the constraint; but iGPU decode on shared LPDDR is bandwidth-limited by the same bus as the CPU, so expect single-digit-percent gains at best and a large variance in reported numbers — this is unverified here and should be measured, not assumed. NPU paths (OpenVINO on Meteor Lake/Lunar Lake class parts) require model conversion and often a fixed shape; they are a project risk, not a baseline.

## 6. Five candidate gaps

**G1 — A CPU-only quality/RAM/latency Pareto for 0.3B–4B, one fixed task, disclosed protocol.** The cheapest experiment that still has statistical power: two models (Llama-3.2-1B, Llama-3.2-3B) × three formats (Q4_K_M, Q5_K_M, Q8_0), GSM8K's full 1,319-item test split plus a 150-prompt IFEval subset, two repeats, fixed thread count, fixed seed, warmup discarded, and peak RSS sampled from the process tree rather than read off the file size. On the throughput estimates in Section 5 that is roughly 15–30 model-hours — three to five overnight runs, not one. A 250-item GSM8K subsample would cut that by 80% and is not worth it: at n=250 the 95% Wilson interval is about ±6 points, which cannot separate a 4-bit model from its 8-bit twin, while the full split gives ±2.6. The confound: quantisation and file size are collinear with model size, so a frontier over "models × formats" cannot attribute a quality change to quantisation; report within-model deltas separately from across-model comparison, as [5] does.

**G2 — Does the quality cliff move with model size?** The claim "4-bit is safe, 3-bit is not" is established at 7–8B [5] and assumed everywhere else. Cheapest test: Llama-3.2-1B vs 3B vs Qwen3-1.7B at Q3_K_S/Q4_K_M/Q5_K_M on the same 500-item GSM8K subsample, 3 seeds. Confound: small models sit closer to the floor on GSM8K, so an absolute-point comparison flatters them; report relative degradation and also a task where the 1B is not near floor (IFEval), or the result will be an artefact of headroom.

**G3 — Is perplexity a valid proxy at the small end?** [5] shows it is not at 8B; nobody has replicated that at 1–3B, where the same 0.1-PPL difference is a much larger fraction of the signal. Cheapest test: rank ten model×format configurations by WikiText-2 PPL and by GSM8K/IFEval accuracy, then compute the rank correlation. Confound: PPL depends on tokenizer and context window, so cross-family comparison is invalid; restrict to within-family, across-format.

**G4 — Speculative decoding on CPU: is the crossover real?** [16] predicts CPU verification is serial at small batch; that prediction is untested on x86. Cheapest test: llama.cpp `-md` draft (Qwen3-0.6B) against Qwen3-1.7B target, sweep K ∈ {1,2,4,8}, measure tok/s and acceptance at fixed thread count; add a synthetic check of whether verify cost scales with batch by timing a single n_batch pass at increasing sizes. Confound: llama.cpp's draft path changes batch size internally, so "K" as configured is not "K" as executed; instrument rather than trust the flag.

**G5 — What does "2 GB" mean, and does anyone's model fit it?** The cheapest useful contribution in this whole area: take three plausible definitions (weight file, llama.cpp allocation at declared context, peak RSS of the process tree), measure all three for six models, and publish the discrepancy. Confound: peak RSS depends on the OS page cache and on whether mmap'd weights count as resident — on Windows this is worse than on Linux, and the honest move is to report both "private working set" and "total including mapped file", since they differ by the entire model size.

ABSTRACT: Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) ranks passages by their relevance to a question and passes the
top ones to the generator whether or not they contain the answer. Relevance, however, does not imply sufficiency:
a passage can be on topic and still lack the fact the question asks for. We place a sufficiency gate between retriever
and generator that judges whether the retrieved context is adequate and then answers, abstains, or retrieves more,
and we compare four scorers built without any sufficiency-labelled training data--a simple heuristic, a single-passage
cross-encoder, a set-level cross-encoder that scores the retrieved passages jointly, and the generator itself prompted
as a judge--on 500 HotpotQA and 500 MuSiQue multi-hop questions with Flan-T5-Large. An answer is counted as
unsupported when no retrieved passage entails it according to a natural language inference model (DeBERTa-large-
MNLI) at a threshold fixed once on held-out data. Because abstaining lowers any error count that ignores unanswered
questions, we report the unsupported rate among answered questions alongside coverage. Vanilla RAG is unsupported
on 26.2% of HotpotQA and 30.8% of MuSiQue answers. At their calibrated operating points the set-level cross-encoder
is the best gate on HotpotQA (17.1% unsupported at 57.2% coverage, with 7.0% of sufficient contexts wrongly refused) and
the generator-as-judge on MuSiQue (27.0% at 71.0% coverage, none wrongly refused). Only the judge beats a random
abstainer matched to its own coverage on both datasets (both p < 0.01), and only the judge ranks best at every threshold.
Reliability is paid for in usefulness: on HotpotQA, Exact Match rises from 32.0% to 43.9% among answered questions
but falls from 32.0% to 25.0% when abstentions are scored as wrong. Both cross-encoders identify insufficient context
better than the heuristic by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, decisively on HotpotQA (0.775
and 0.675 against 0.521) but within the noise on MuSiQue (0.738 and 0.717 against 0.658); by selective risk across all
operating points the heuristic is worse than not gating at all. Replacing the retrieved context with the gold supporting
passages removes about two thirds of the answer-quality failures and between a third and a half of the unsupported
answers, so much of the failure, though not all, originates in retrieval rather than the generator. Repeating the evaluation
with Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct shifts the absolute rates but preserves the ordering of the scorers.

KEYWORDS: Retrieval-augmented generation; context sufficiency; hallucination; selective generation; abstention;
multi-hop question answering; cross-encoder

1 Introduction

     Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) grounds language model outputs in external knowledge by
retrieving passages and conditioning the generator on them [1­3]. One assumption in that pipeline remains
unexamined: that the retrieved passages are sufficient to answer the query. Retrievers rank by relevance--
similarity to the query--and return the top-k without assessing completeness, yet a passage on topic can
score highly while lacking the fact the question asks for. Better embeddings and reranking improve relevance;
nothing addresses sufficiency.

     We measure the resulting failure as the unsupported-answer rate, the share of generated answers not
entailed by any retrieved passage under a natural language inference model, at a threshold fixed once on
held-out data (Section 4.4). Vanilla RAG is unsupported on 26.2% of HotpotQA and 30.8% of MuSiQue
answers. Such rates do not identify a cause: unsupported answers can also arise from reasoning errors,
conflicting evidence, prompt sensitivity, failed evidence integration, or the generator itself. We therefore
test attribution by intervention. Holding generator, prompt and decoding fixed and replacing the retrieved
context with the gold supporting passages removes 68.9% of answer-quality failures on HotpotQA and 68.2%
on MuSiQue, and 48.1% and 34.4% of unsupported answers (Section 5.1). Insufficient context is therefore a
major contributor on both measures and the dominant one for answer quality; what remains survives perfect
evidence and belongs to the generator.

     Rather than improving what is retrieved, we ask whether it is enough. The Sufficiency Gate sits between
retriever and generator, estimates context adequacy before generation, and on judging the context inadequate
either abstains or expands retrieval to a higher k. No sufficiency-labelled data enters the gate. Scoring
sufficiency with a cross-encoder trained for passage relevance on MS MARCO is not a claim that the two
properties coincide, but a use of relevance as a proxy feature, tested against gold supporting-fact labels
rather than assumed. A single-passage relevance score separates sufficient from insufficient contexts with
an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.675 on HotpotQA and 0.717 on
MuSiQue; scoring the retrieved set jointly instead of taking the best single passage--the same model, only
the aggregation changed--raises this to 0.775 and 0.738 (Section 5.3). Relevance therefore carries real but
partial signal, which is why we also report a generator-as-judge scorer with no relevance training.

     Because a gate can lower the unsupported rate simply by abstaining, we report reliability and usefulness
together: coverage, abstention and false-abstention rates, the unsupported rate among answered queries as
well as with abstentions credited as supported, Exact Match over answered and over all queries, and risk­
coverage curves. The gate is also compared with generator confidence, with classical query-performance
predictors, and with a Self-RAG-style pipeline (Sections 3.5 and 5.9). The contributions are as follows:

1. We separate retrieval relevance from context sufficiency and measure, by intervention, how much of the
     failure each explains.

2. We specify the Sufficiency Gate, an inference-time module that needs no retraining and no sufficiency
     labels, and state what a deployment must supply to run it.

3. We compare four scorers under one protocol: set-level aggregation gains +0.100 AUROC on HotpotQA
     (95% CI [+0.069, +0.132]), and the generator-as-judge alone beats a random abstainer matched to its
     own rate on both datasets.

4. We show that crediting abstentions as non-hallucinations inflates the apparent reduction, and give the
     protocol that separates artefact from effect.

5. We report the negative results: by selective risk the heuristic is worse than not gating on both datasets, its
     context-length term reduces discrimination monotonically, and the calibration percentile is a coverage
     control, not a learned boundary.

2 Related Work

2.1 Retrieval-Augmented Generation

     Every component of a modern RAG pipeline is a transformer [4]: the encoder that embeds queries
and passages, the cross-encoder that scores them jointly, and the generator that conditions on the result.
Bidirectional and sequence-to-sequence pre-training [5,6] is what allows a model to be conditioned at
inference time on arbitrary passages, and is therefore the precondition for retrieval augmentation rather
than an implementation detail. The gate studied here inherits the same lineage: its scorers are transformer
rerankers, and its strongest variant is the generator itself.

     RAG was formalised by Lewis et al. [1], who trained a dense retriever and a sequence-to-sequence
generator end to end; REALM [7] pre-trains a retriever jointly with a masked language model, and Atlas [8]
scales joint retriever­generator training to few-shot knowledge-intensive tasks. A parallel line improves
retrieval alone--better encoders [9], zero-shot dense retrieval [10], hybrid sparse­dense search [11] and
multi-stage reranking [12]--while others restructure what is retrieved, or how often: GraphRAG [13]
organises the corpus as a knowledge graph, RAPTOR [14] retrieves over a recursive summary tree, and
IRCoT [15] interleaves retrieval with chain-of-thought reasoning, with toolkits such as FlashRAG [16]
standardising pipeline construction.

     These approaches all treat retrieval as a ranking problem and pass the result to the generator without an
explicit adequacy check, so none establishes that the retrieved evidence is enough. The gate is orthogonal to
this line rather than in competition with it: any retriever above can sit upstream of it unchanged.

2.2 Hallucination and its Measurement

     The generation of fluent but factually incorrect text has been studied extensively [17­19], including
through internal signals such as semantic entropy, which detects confabulated outputs from the model's
own uncertainty [20]. FActScore [21] decomposes long-form generations into atomic facts and verifies each
against a knowledge source. Measures of this kind act after generation rather than intervening before it.

     In the retrieval setting, grounding a generator in retrieved evidence reduces hallucination relative to
parametric-only generation [2], but that result presumes the evidence is adequate. RAGTruth [22] documents
the residual case directly, annotating spans of RAG output that the retrieved content does not support. What
such corpora leave open--whether the retrieved passages were adequate to begin with--is the question this
paper takes up. Our measurement follows the same evidence-grounded principle--an answer is unsupported
when no retrieved passage entails it (Section 4.4)--so that reliability is judged against what the system
actually retrieved rather than against a reference string.

2.3 Selective Generation and Abstention

     Abstaining when a model is uncertain is a long-standing idea in machine learning [23]. In question
answering, Kamath et al. [24] abstain on generator confidence, and Mallen et al. [25] invoke retrieval only
when parametric confidence falls below a calibrated threshold.

     Self-RAG [26] takes the stronger route of retraining the generator. The model learns to emit special
reflection tokens: a retrieval token that calls the retriever on demand, and critique tokens that rate whether
a retrieved passage is relevant, whether the generated text is supported by it, and how useful the response is;
these tokens then weigh candidate continuations during decoding, so retrieval and self-assessment become
4                                                                        Comput Mater Contin. 2026

part of generation itself. The supervision for them is distilled from a separate critic model, so the capability
arrives with a retraining cost. We compare against it empirically in Section 5.9. FLARE [27] needs no training
and works during generation instead: it drafts the upcoming sentence and re-retrieves when that draft
contains low-confidence tokens.

     Three systems bracket the position our gate occupies. Corrective RAG [28] is the closest architecturally,
placing a retrieval evaluator between retrieval and generation, mapping its confidence onto correct, incorrect
and ambiguous actions, and expanding retrieval through web search when the set is judged inadequate--
the same slot in the pipeline and the same repertoire of actions, but with an evaluator fine-tuned for the
role. Adaptive-RAG [29] also decides before generation, yet routes on predicted question complexity rather
than on evidence adequacy. HALT-RAG [30] acts afterwards, flagging unsupported outputs with a calibrated
ensemble of frozen NLI models.

     What separates the present work is not where it acts but what it requires. Corrective RAG fine-tunes
an evaluator, Adaptive-RAG trains a router, Self-RAG retrains the generator, and the selective-generation
method of Joren et al. [31] fits a trained head over an autorater; each needs a trained component and labelled
data to fit it. The gate evaluated here needs neither, and its strongest variant is a prompt issued to the generator
already present in the pipeline. Table 1 places these systems side by side.

2.4 Context Sufficiency and the Relevance Gap

     Several studies examine the adequacy of retrieved evidence without gating on it. Adlakha et al. [32]
relate context faithfulness to answer quality, and Yoran et al. [33] show that retrieval can actively harm multi-
hop accuracy when irrelevant passages are blended with relevant ones, testing an NLI entailment filter as one
mitigation. RAGAS [34] and ARES [35] score pipelines post hoc along axes such as context relevance and
faithfulness, as diagnostic frameworks rather than inference-time interventions.
Comput Mater Contin. 2026                  5

     Our gate differs from the NLI line in what the scorer is asked to judge. An NLI model is trained on
declarative premise­hypothesis pairs [36]. A generated answer is such a hypothesis, which is why entailment
serves well as our hallucination measure; a question is not, so asking an NLI model whether a passage entails
a question puts it outside the distribution it was trained on. A cross-encoder trained for passage relevance,
by contrast, takes exactly the question­passage input the gate has available. Relevance is therefore used as a
proxy feature for sufficiency, never as a measure of it, and Section 5.3 reports how far that proxy carries.

     Joren et al. [31] is the work closest to ours in framing. It defines context sufficiency--whether the
context contains enough information to give a definitive answer, a criterion that does not require the gold
answer to apply--and classifies it before generation with an LLM autorater prompted to reason step by
step, alongside cheaper alternatives including an entailment-based rater and fine-tuned open models. Their
selective generation combines the sufficiency label with the model's self-rated confidence through a trained
head, improving the fraction of correct answers among answered queries by 2 to 10 points across proprietary
and open generators.

     We adopt that notion of sufficiency and ask what remains of it when the trained parts are removed. Three
consequences follow. The sufficiency signal comes from the generator already in the pipeline rather than from
a separate rater, so no labelled sufficiency data is needed anywhere. The scoring model is held fixed while
only the aggregation changes, which isolates what set-level scoring contributes. And every gate is measured
against a random abstainer matched to its own coverage, which establishes whether a gate discriminates at
all rather than which of two gates is better. The generators evaluated here are also an order of magnitude
smaller than those in that work.

     The gap is especially consequential in three settings: multi-hop questions, where the answer must
be assembled across passages and no single one holds it; long-tail queries, where the corpus discusses
an entity at length without stating the date, quantity or relation requested; and distractor pools such as
HotpotQA's [37], seeded with high-similarity passages that do not contain the answer. In each the generator
receives a plausible-looking context, answers confidently, and gives the user no signal that it is unreliable.

     A better retriever narrows this gap without closing it: when the corpus does not hold the fact, a stronger
encoder still returns the best available passages, which may be relevant and still insufficient. The problem
also survives successful retrieval, since models underuse relevant information placed in the middle of long
contexts [38], and context compression [39] is itself motivated by the observation that more retrieved text
does not become better-used context. The complementary intervention is to detect that retrieval has failed
and withhold generation, rather than assume it always succeeds.

3 Materials and Methods

     Let q be a query and C = {p1, . . . , pk} the passages a retriever R returns from corpus D, so that a standard
pipeline produces a = G(q, C) for a generator G. We call C sufficient for q when it contains the information
needed to answer q, and insufficient otherwise. Nothing in the standard pipeline tests that property before
invoking G, so an insufficient context is passed on unchallenged. We therefore insert a sufficiency gate.

3.1 Heuristic Gate
     The cheapest possible gate combines two signals that retrieval has already produced:

sheuristic = 0.6 × si¯m(q, C) × 0.4 × len_norm(C)                                          (3)

where si¯m is the mean cosine similarity between the query and the retrieved passages as returned by the
retriever, and len_norm(C) = min(w, 512)/512 with w the number of whitespace-delimited tokens in C; the
score is clipped to [0, 1]. Both terms are already on a fixed scale, so the threshold is fixed at  = 0.50 for both
datasets and never calibrated, and the gate adds no model call at all. It is reported as a floor rather than a
recommendation: Section 5.8 shows the length term makes it worse at every weight tested.

3.2 Single-Passage Cross-Encoder Gate (MS-MARCO)

     The first learned scorer replaces that heuristic with relevance from cross-encoder/ms-marco-
MiniLM-L-6-v2, a 6-layer cross-encoder trained on question­passage pairs labelled for relevance on MS
MARCO [40]. Each passage is truncated to 512 characters, encoded jointly with the query, and scored; the
gate takes the maximum:

   i  {1,...,k}

     The maximum carries over from single-hop retrieval an assumption we do not defend, that one highly
rated passage is all the generator needs; Section 3.3 tests it directly. Scores are used as raw logits, since sigmoid

or softmax normalisation compresses them into a range where good and poor contexts are hard to separate
by a threshold.

     Relevance is used as a proxy feature for sufficiency, never as a measure of it. An NLI model is not the
alternative it appears to be, since it expects a declarative hypothesis and a question is not one; that is why NLI
scores our generated answers instead (Section 4.4) while the gate uses a scorer built for question­passage
input. Whether a relevance-trained scorer can separate irrelevant, relevant but incomplete, and genuinely
sufficient contexts is settled against gold supporting-fact labels in Section 5.3.

3.3 Set-Level Cross-Encoder Gate (Concat-CE)

     Multi-hop evidence is spread across passages, so a maximum reports the best fragment rather than
the adequacy of the set. The set-level variant scores the passages together, as one string, which is what the
generator will actually see:

sgate = CrossEncoder (q, p1  p2      pk)                        (5)

where  is concatenation and the joined context is truncated to 1500 characters. The model is unchanged
and only the granularity of its input differs, so any change in behaviour is attributable to aggregation rather
than to the scorer.

     This also repairs a structural weakness in the expansion path. A maximum over a larger set cannot fall,
so a single-passage gate can never register that expanding retrieval added nothing useful. A set-level score
can fall, because irrelevant material dilutes the concatenated context, which allows an expanded context to
stay below threshold.

3.4 Generator-as-Judge Gate (LLM-Judge)

     Both cross-encoders score relevance, because that is what their training objective provides. The fourth
scorer asks about sufficiency directly, of the model that will have to use the context. The retrieved passages,
truncated to 2000 characters, are presented with the query and the instruction "Does the context contain ALL
the information needed to answer the question? Answer yes or no." One token is decoded and the gate score is
the probability of yes renormalised over the yes and no logits at that position:

sgate  =       exp zyes       ,  z = logits(q, p1  p2      pk)  (6)
          exp zyes + exp zno

     No model is loaded that the pipeline does not already host and no training data is required, so the cost
is one extra forward pass. What separates this scorer from the cross-encoders is therefore what it measures,
not how it aggregates.

Truncation budgets.

     The three learned scorers intentionally see different amounts of text: Max-CE truncates each passage
to 512 characters, Concat-CE the joined context to 1500, and the LLM-judge the joined context to 2000.
These budgets follow each scorer's input format rather than a shared length. A shared-budget sweep at
L  {512, 1500, 2000} is reported with the second-generator checks in Section 5.9: at the paper's operating
lengths the scorer ordering is stable, while a short budget (L = 512) hurts the judge on MuSiQue, which is
why it is given the longer context.

3.5 Baselines and Controls

Generator confidence.

     Thresholding the generator's own confidence is the standard baseline in selective prediction and an
essential comparison for this work: if a score the generator already produces buys the same reliability, a
separate gate must justify its cost. The answer is generated first under the same four-beam decoding as every
other configuration, scored by the length-normalised log-probability of the returned beam, and withdrawn
below the 40th percentile of its own score distribution. That scale is not comparable with a cross-encoder
logit, so every comparison against it in Section 5.5 is made at matched coverage. It is a filter on the output,
not a gate on the input, so it saves no generation cost.

Query-performance predictors.

     Information retrieval has asked a related question since long before RAG: given a query, will this
retrieval go well? Its predictors [41] are training-free and nearly costless, so they set the bar a sufficiency
scorer must clear. Pre-retrieval predictors see only the query and collection statistics (average and maximum
inverse document frequency, simplified clarity score, average and maximum collection query similarity,
query length); post-retrieval predictors also see the retrieved list (mean and maximum similarity, score
standard deviation, clarity [42], weighted information gain [43], normalised query commitment [44], score
magnitude and variance). All are computed on the same queries and labels as the gates. Three approximations
bound their interpretation: term statistics come from the union of retrieved passages rather than the full
index, the background estimate for weighted information gain and normalised query commitment is the top
six passages, and clarity uses Jelinek­Mercer smoothing at  = 0.6 over the top three. The first two make
these predictors conservative rather than flattered.

Random abstention.

     Every gate is also compared against a control withholding the same number of queries, chosen at random
rather than by score. A gate that does not discriminate lands on this control however much it abstains, so
clearing it is the minimum evidence that sufficiency estimation is doing any work; the construction and
permutation test are given in Section 4.4.

Oracle context.

     A difference in failure between sufficient and insufficient contexts is an association: the generator might
be failing because evidence was missing, or failing on those same queries for its own reasons. Separating the
two requires an intervention, so the generator is run twice on every query--once on the retrieved passages
and once on the gold supporting passages with retrieval bypassed--holding generator, prompt and decoding
fixed. Because retrieval is the only thing that changes, the difference is the share of failure attributable to it,
and what remains belongs to the generator. We report the decomposition on both failure measures, since
they do not give the same share.

Controlled Self-RAG implementation.

     The Self-RAG comparison in Section 5.9 uses the released 7B checkpoint at k = 3 in 4-bit quantisation,
confounding gating strategy with model size and precision. We therefore reimplemented Self-RAG's critique-
and-select mechanism on Flan-T5-Large in FP16 at k = 6, changing only the gating strategy. Results are
reported in Section 5.12.

3.7 Re-Retrieve Strategy

     Rather than abstain immediately, the gate may expand retrieval once. The re-retrieve variant doubles
the budget to k = 6, re-scores the expanded context C = R(q, 6), and invokes the generator only if that score
clears the same , with no extra margin demanded of the second look:

a = G,(q, C),  if f (q, C) = sufficient  (8)
               otherwise

     A worked case makes the branches of Fig. 1 concrete. For a two-hop question asking which of two
magazines was founded first, suppose the top three passages cover only the first magazine. The gate scores
that set once and falls below , the second founding date being absent. The abstain-only configuration stops
there and never invokes the generator. The re-retrieve configuration retrieves again at k = 6 and re-scores: if
the missing article arrived, generation proceeds on all six passages; if not, the system abstains, having spent
one retrieval and one gate call but no generation. Section 5.8 reports how often each branch is taken and how
often a reversal is warranted.

4 Experimental Setup

4.1 Datasets and Corpora
     Both benchmarks are multi-hop, which is the setting in which relevance and sufficiency come apart most

sharply. HotpotQA [37] requires reasoning over two Wikipedia passages; we use the distractor configuration
10  Comput Mater Contin. 2026

of the validation split, in which ten passages, only two of them supporting, accompany each question.
MuSiQue [45] requires two to four hops and is constructed to eliminate questions answerable from a single
passage; we use the answerable validation split.

     Each corpus is built deterministically from the benchmark's own material. For each dataset we take the
first 700 validation queries, pool every passage the benchmark distributes with them--all ten paragraphs
per HotpotQA question with the title prepended, every paragraph in the MuSiQue paragraph list--then
deduplicate, sort lexicographically and keep the first 5000 entries. Sorting before truncation is what makes the
corpus reproducible, since set iteration order is not stable across runs even under a fixed seed. No chunking
is applied: each passage is one benchmark paragraph, indexed whole. Nothing is added to either corpus--no
external text, no answer strings, no generated material--so the only contamination risk runs the other way,
the 5000-passage cap discarding gold evidence, which Section 5.8 quantifies.

     The two datasets are not equally hard, and the gap should be read before any result. Under an identical
pipeline, vanilla RAG reaches 32.0% Exact Match on HotpotQA and 8.4% on MuSiQue. MuSiQue's longer
chains mean more passages must all be present, its construction removes exactly the single-passage cases a
top-k retriever handles well, and its wider answer space makes a partially correct chain less likely to land
on the reference string. Sufficiency rates follow: at k = 3, 22.6% of HotpotQA contexts are sufficient against
5.0% of MuSiQue. Absolute values should therefore never be compared across datasets; only the ordering of
methods within a dataset is meaningful.

Corpus size and sample size.
     The evaluation samples n = 500 questions per dataset, a scale consistent with the validation-set

evaluations reported for the underlying benchmarks [37,45]. It gives adequate precision on the primary
reliability measure: 95% confidence intervals on the unsupported-answer rate span roughly ±2­5 percentage
points throughout Section 5.2. Depth was preferred to size at this scale--stratifying by retrieval quality and
by oracle-evidence availability within the corpus (Section 5.10), and adding two out-of-domain datasets, MS
MARCO and SQuAD 2.0 (Section 5.11). Section 4.6 reports the power this sample size affords.

4.2 Models and Infrastructure

     Table 2 lists every component. Retrieval uses FAISS IndexFlatIP, an exact inner-product index
with no quantisation, so retrieval introduces no approximation error. The generator decodes with four
beams (num_beams = 4) and early stopping to a maximum of 64 tokens. Four-beam search is not greedy
decoding, and the distinction matters because the generator-confidence baseline of Section 3.5 scores the
length-normalised log-probability of the returned beam. The LLM-judge decodes differently--one beam and
one token--since it reads the probability of yes against no at the first position rather than generating text.

4.3 Pipeline Configurations

     Seven pipeline configurations are evaluated, plus the generator-confidence baseline (Table 3). Because
that baseline filters answers after generation rather than gating before it, and because its score is on an
incomparable scale, it appears in the discrimination and matched-coverage analyses rather than as a row in
the main tables.

4.4 Evaluation Metrics

     Five quantities are reported per configuration. Exact Match and Token F1 compare the predicted answer
with the gold answer; both are reported twice, over answered queries and over all 500 with abstentions scored
incorrect, because a gated and an ungated pipeline do not answer the same set. The unsupported-answer
rate is the share of generated answers that no retrieved passage entails, judged by DeBERTa-large-MNLI with
each passage as premise and the answer as hypothesis; an answer counts as supported when the maximum
entailment probability across passages exceeds 0.250, a threshold fixed once on the calibration set and never
retuned. The answer-quality failure rate is the share of answers scoring Token F1 below 0.10. The abstention
rate is the share of queries the gate declines.

     We deliberately do not call the answer-quality failure rate a hallucination rate. Low overlap with a
reference means disagreement with that reference, which is not the same as an ungrounded answer: an
answer faithful to the retrieved passages can score poorly against the reference, and an answer overlapping
the reference can be supported by nothing retrieved. Since the claim of this paper concerns grounding, the
entailment measure is primary and Token F1 is kept as a separate answer-quality measure. One definition,
one threshold and one procedure are applied to every configuration, vanilla RAG included.

the area beneath (AURC, lower is better). A scorer with no discriminative power yields a flat curve at the
base rate, so its AURC equals the vanilla unsupported rate--the reference against which every scorer should
be read.

4.5 Sufficiency Labels

     Judging whether a gate estimates sufficiency requires ground truth for the question did retrieval return
the evidence a reader would need? We label a retrieved set sufficient when it contains every gold supporting
sentence the benchmark's annotators marked for that query, and for MuSiQue when every single-hop
decomposition step is covered as well. Sufficiency is thus a property of the set rather than of any passage,
which is what a multi-hop benchmark is built to test.

     The obvious alternative, answer-string containment, is rejected. It cannot certify that a full evidence
chain is present, and it misclassifies paraphrased, implicit and compositional evidence--the cases MuSiQue
is made of. The two criteria disagree substantially: answer-string containment calls twice as many HotpotQA
contexts and more than three times as many MuSiQue contexts sufficient, at only fair agreement (Table 5).
Every sufficiency computation in this paper uses supporting-fact labels.

     What the labels mean should be read precisely. Supporting facts are the sentences from which the
gold answer follows, annotated with the answer in view but not the answer itself, so a set can contain
every supporting sentence and no answer string--common when the answer must be composed across
hops or inferred from a date or comparison. The labels therefore record whether the evidence was retrieved,
not whether the answer was. Being derived from an annotation of what is needed rather than from an
independent rating, they inherit that annotation's incompleteness; Section 6.3 reports their agreement with
direct human judgement.

4.6 Statistical Procedure

     Neither a paired t-test nor McNemar's test is used, and both omissions are deliberate. A t-test is
inappropriate on a binary indicator. McNemar's test, the conventional choice for paired binary outcomes, is
degenerate here: in an abstain-only configuration an answered query receives exactly the ungated response,
so its label cannot change, and a query can change status only by being abstained from. One discordant cell is
therefore zero by construction--c = 0 in all twelve gated configurations, with b between 58 and 86--leaving
the statistic equal to the abstention count, which would declare significance for a gate abstaining at random.

     We therefore test what distinguishes a gate from a coin: whether the queries it withholds contain
more unsupported answers than a random set of the same size. That is a one-sided permutation test over
10,000 draws, reported per configuration in Section 5.5. Scorer-to-scorer comparisons use paired bootstrap
confidence intervals over 5000 stratified resamples, since a difference in AUROC needs an interval to
be interpretable.

Confidence intervals.

     Section 5.2 and the analyses of Sections 5.10­5.12 report 95% confidence intervals for the unsupported-
answer rate and other proportions: Wilson-score where only a percentage and its answered-query count
are needed, bootstrap (10,000 resamples) where per-record values are available. These quantify estimation
precision independently of the permutation test's significance verdict.

Statistical power.

     A post-hoc power analysis, treating each gate-vanilla comparison on the unsupported-answer rate as a
two-proportion test, finds low power at n = 500: Cohen's h ranges from 0.02 (Max-CE) to 0.08 (LLM-judge)
on MuSiQue, an average power of 14% against the conventional 80% threshold, with a minimum detectable
effect of approximately 4.1 percentage points at a 30% baseline rate. The MuSiQue reductions observed here
(0.9­3.8pp) sit at or below that, while HotpotQA's larger reductions fare better. This is why the significance
claims do not rest on that test: the one-sided permutation test above compares a gate's selection against a
size-matched random abstainer directly, rather than relying on the normal approximation such a calculation
assumes, and it returns p = 0.0034 for the LLM-judge on MuSiQue and p < 0.001 on HotpotQA (Section 5.5).
The power figures bound that test's sensitivity, not the permutation result.

Effect sizes.

     Hedge's g (bias-corrected Cohen's d) was also computed for each learned gate's answered-subset
outcomes against vanilla RAG at k = 3. On MuSiQue the magnitudes are small: g = 0.02­0.08 for the
unsupported-answer rate and g = 0.07­0.10 for Exact Match. On HotpotQA the unsupported-rate effects
are larger (g = 0.20­0.22) while remaining inside Cohen's small band; Exact Match spans g = 0.08­0.25.
A small standardised effect size and a significant permutation test are not in tension: the former measures a
mean difference against within-group variance on a noisy binary outcome, while the latter asks whether the
14  Comput Mater Contin. 2026

gate's chosen subset differs from a random one of the same size. We do not claim the hallucination reduction
is large; we claim, per Section 5.5, that it is not attributable to withholding an arbitrary subset of queries.

4.7 Reproducibility

     The 700 queries per dataset are split once: the first 500 form the evaluation set and the remaining
200 the calibration set. Every threshold in this paper is fixed on the calibration set--the 40th-percentile
gate thresholds and the 0.250 entailment threshold alike--then applied unchanged to the evaluation set. No
threshold is tuned on evaluation data at any point. The global seed is 42.

     The generator prompt is "Answer the question using only the context.\n\
nContext: {context}\n\nQuestion: {question}\n\nAnswer:" and the judge prompt
is "Context: {context}\n\nQuestion: {question}\n\nDoes the context contain
ALL the information needed to answer the question? Answer yes or no." In
both, the concatenated context is truncated to 2000 characters and the tokenised input to 768 tokens; for
the entailment metric each premise passage is truncated to 1200 characters and 512 tokens.

     The generator runs in half precision on the GPU while the entailment model is held in single precision,
because half precision shifts its entailment probabilities enough to move the calibrated threshold. All
experiments ran on one NVIDIA T4 (Google Colab Pro) with PyTorch 2.11.0 and CUDA 12.8 under Linux.

5 Results

     The evaluation is organised as twelve findings. The first locates where the failure originates and the
second how much of the apparent remedy is real; Findings 3 to 7 test the four scorers as rankers and then
as gates, against the free alternatives retrieval already provides and against matched controls; Findings 8 and
9 cover the design variants and the external checks; Findings 10 to 12 ask where inside the corpus the gate
acts, what survives a change of domain, and how the gate compares against a trained system under matched
conditions. Findings 1 to 9 use the primary retrieval depth k = 3; Findings 10 to 12 read the expanded k = 6
field and state that depth where they report rates. All values are computed over 500 queries per dataset under
the protocol of Section 4.

5.1 Finding 1: Insufficient Retrieval Accounts for Most of the Failure

     Vanilla RAG leaves 26.2% of HotpotQA and 30.8% of MuSiQue answers unsupported, and it fails more
often when the retrieved context is insufficient (Fig. 3). That association does not establish a cause, since
the same queries may be harder for the generator in their own right, so it is replaced by an intervention:
generator, prompt and decoding are held fixed while the retrieved context is swapped for the gold supporting
passages (Section 3.5).

     Gold context moves everything. Answer-quality failure falls from 50.8% to 15.8% on HotpotQA and
from 78.0% to 24.8% on MuSiQue, with Exact Match rising from 32.0% to 62.0% and from 8.4% to 54.4%.
The unsupported rate falls more modestly, from 26.2% to 13.6% and from 30.8% to 20.2%. As shares (Table 6),
the substitution removes 68.9% and 68.2% of answer-quality failure and 48.1% and 34.4% of unsupported
answers. Retrieval is therefore the largest single source of failure, which is what recommends a gate at that
point in the pipeline; and because 13.6% and 20.2% of answers remain unentailed with the gold evidence in
the prompt, a gate that judged sufficiency perfectly would still leave roughly a third of the failures (Fig. 4).
That residue bounds every result below.

the control.

5.5 Finding 5: Only the Generator-as-Judge Beats Matched Random Abstention

     Whether a gate discriminates at all is tested against an abstainer of the same size choosing at random,
by the one-sided permutation test of Section 4.6. The judge reaches 17.2% against a control of 26.2% with a
95% interval of [22.8, 29.5] on HotpotQA (p < 0.001), and 27.0% against 30.8% with [28.2, 33.2] on MuSiQue
(p = 0.0034). It is the only configuration clearing the control on both: the cross-encoders clear it on HotpotQA
only, and the heuristic on neither.

     False abstention agrees. On HotpotQA the judge refuses 4.2% of queries whose context was in fact
sufficient, against 7.0% for set-level scoring, 10.0% for single-passage scoring and 22.0% for the heuristic; on
MuSiQue it refuses none. Its advantage therefore comes from which queries it withholds rather than from
withholding more of them: on HotpotQA it abstains on 43.0% of queries against 42.8% for set-level scoring
and 48.2% for the heuristic.

5.6 Finding 6: Generator Confidence Does Not Substitute for a Sufficiency Gate

     Confidence-based selective prediction is nearly free, so it sets a second bar the gate must clear. At
matched 60% coverage on HotpotQA it leaves 20.7% of returned answers unsupported against 17.0% for the
judge, and wrongly withholds 14.5% of sufficient contexts against 4.0%; on MuSiQue, 27.7% against 26.0%
with false abstention 7.5% against 0.5%. Its AUROC is 0.633 on HotpotQA and 0.441 on MuSiQue, where
the point estimate falls below chance on an interval of [0.326, 0.562] that contains it, so on MuSiQue the
generator's confidence carries no usable signal about sufficiency in either direction. One result runs the other
way: on HotpotQA confidence gives the highest Exact Match among answered queries, 44.3% against 42.3%.
Confidence tracks whether an answer is right, not whether it is grounded.

     Because a single threshold can favour a scorer by chance, the comparison is repeated at 50%, 60% and
70% coverage (Table 13, Fig. 8). The judge wins five of the six unsupported-rate columns and varies by only 0.2
points on HotpotQA and 1.1 on MuSiQue across the sweep. The ordering is stable on HotpotQA throughout
and on MuSiQue except at the lowest coverage, where confidence reaches 24.4% against the judge's 26.4%--
one cell of the sweep, from a scorer whose MuSiQue ranking is indistinguishable from chance and which
fails the random control.

5.7 Finding 7: The Ordering Holds at Every Threshold

     Integrating over all thresholds removes the last dependence on a chosen operating point
(Table 14, Fig. 9). On HotpotQA every learned scorer falls below the random-abstention reference of 26.2--
the judge at 17.54, confidence 18.50, set-level 18.67, single-passage 18.89--while the heuristic reaches 28.49,
worse than choosing at random. On MuSiQue, against 30.8: judge 24.29, confidence 27.35, set-level 28.89,
single-passage 30.71, heuristic 33.34. Two conclusions follow that no single operating point can give: the
judge is the strongest scorer at every threshold on both datasets, and the heuristic has no usable operating
point at all.

5.8 Finding 8: Three Design Choices That Do Not Behave as Intended

The threshold sets coverage, not a sufficiency boundary.

     Sweeping the percentile from the 25th to the 55th for both cross-encoder gates moves two columns and
leaves one flat (Table 15). Coverage tracks the percentile almost perfectly (correlation -0.99 or stronger in
all four series), and the credited rate nearly halves with it, from 13.2% to 7.0% on HotpotQA and 23.0% to
15.6% on MuSiQue. The conditional rate barely moves: 1.6 points on HotpotQA and 0.7 on MuSiQue, against
coverage swings of 30.2 and 24.0. Since raising the percentile reorders no scores, discrimination is untouched
and only the crediting convention responds. The 40th percentile is therefore a coverage setting, not a learned
boundary, and gates must be compared at matched coverage rather than at a matched percentile.

The length term in the heuristic score is harmful.

     Sweeping wcos from 0.0 (length only) to 1.0 (similarity only) with everything else fixed raises AUROC
at every step, from 0.378 to 0.695 on HotpotQA and 0.559 to 0.731 on MuSiQue (Table 16). Length alone
scores 0.378, an inverted ranking, so longer contexts are if anything less likely to be sufficient. At wcos = 0.6,
the setting used elsewhere in this paper, AUROC is 0.521 and 0.658, so the length term costs roughly 0.17
and 0.07 and should be removed. Because the control, permutation test and risk­coverage analysis were not
repeated at wcos = 1.0, the claim concerns the length term rather than the merits of pure similarity as a gate.

Re-retrieval recovers almost nothing, and the corpus explains why.

     Expanding from k = 3 to k = 6 before deciding leaves the conditional unsupported rate essentially
unchanged--17.8% against 18.0% for single-passage scoring on HotpotQA, 17.1% against 17.1% for set-level
scoring, and 29.9% against 29.9% on MuSiQue--while coverage rises by about two points. Table 17 shows
why. Only some scorers can register a useless expansion at all: the top-6 set contains the whole top-3 set on
all 500 queries of both datasets, so maximum aggregation is monotone and single-passage scores never fall,
whereas set-level scores fall on 30.0% and 32.4% of queries and the judge on 42.0% and 46.2%--the property
argued for in Section 3.3. Nor are the reversals warranted: set-level scoring flips on 3.3% of below-threshold
HotpotQA queries and is right 57.1% of the time, while the heuristic flips on 92.5% and is right 4.9%, treating
any new text as sufficient. On MuSiQue no scorer flips usefully.

a capability (Section 6.3).

Unanswerable questions.

     SQuAD 2.0's unanswerable split puts the question directly: no answer is supportable, so a gate that
detects insufficiency should refuse. Under the same unmodified threshold it abstains on 498 of 500 queries
(99.6%, [98.6, 99.9]), and neither of the two answers it returns is flagged unsupported. Ungated, the pipeline
answers all 500, of which 36.2% [32.0, 40.6] are flagged--and because none of these questions has a correct

here is measured on abstention itself rather than inferred from a hallucination rate.

     The sufficiency signal therefore moves in the right direction out of domain while the threshold placed
on it does not: the operating point has to be re-calibrated per domain.

6 Discussion

6.1 What the Findings Support
     Three claims follow from the findings above, and each carries a boundary. The first is that the reduction

usually visible in a gated pipeline is largely an accounting effect. Crediting abstentions as supported moves
Comput Mater Contin. 2026  31

the unsupported rate by 13 to 17 points on these datasets; measured over the answers actually given, the same
gates move it by at most 9 points on HotpotQA and 4 on MuSiQue, and the heuristic moves it the wrong
way. Reporting the conditional rate against a matched random control is what separates the two, and on that
test only the generator prompted as a judge survives on both datasets.

     The second is that sufficiency can be estimated without any sufficiency-labelled data. Relevance is a
partial proxy: a cross-encoder trained for passage ranking separates sufficient from insufficient contexts
well above chance, and how its scores are aggregated matters more than which model produces them. The
generator's own judgement is better still and costs one forward pass, but it needs token-probability access, so
the set-level cross-encoder is the portable choice; the two differ by 0.011 and 0.035 AUROC and by more than
an order of magnitude in latency. Reading the same scorers by area under the risk­coverage curve rather
than at one operating point guards against a scorer that happens to look best where the threshold was placed;
the two views agree here, with the single exception of generator confidence on MuSiQue, second-best by
AURC despite an AUROC no better than chance.

     The third is that the ceiling is set by retrieval and by the corpus rather than by the gate, so the levels
reported here are properties of this setup while the ordering of the scorers is the part expected to transfer.
Whether the trade is worth making--more reliable answers, fewer of them--is a deployment question rather
than an empirical one, which the next section makes concrete.

6.2 Deployment Requirements and Measured Cost

     The gate is a stateless scoring function inserted between retrieval and generation. It changes neither the
vector database, nor the retriever, nor the language model. It is not, however, free of prerequisites, and four
should be stated plainly:

· A calibration set from production traffic. The threshold is a percentile of an observed score distri-
     bution, so it transfers neither from this paper nor from another corpus, and per Section 5.8 the value
     chosen fixes how often the system declines.

· A stated price for a refusal relative to a wrong answer. The gate implements an operating point; it does
     not choose one. Population-level Exact Match falls even as the answers given get more accurate.

· A downstream path for abstentions--escalation, live search, a clarifying question. A refusal with no
     fallback converts a wrong answer into a dead end.

· Token-probability access, if the judge is used. A hosted endpoint returning only text cannot produce
     P(yes); the set-level cross-encoder is then the strongest remaining option.

     Costs are measured rather than estimated (Table 25). The cross-encoders cost about 3% of a generation
call and are effectively free, which makes them the portable choice despite discriminating less well. The judge
adds roughly 40% to the time to answer and is the least predictable gate, but that figure prices the gate as
though generation always followed it. It does not: abstention skips generation entirely, so at the coverage levels
reported here the judge's added cost and the generation it avoids nearly cancel. Operationally, the abstention
rate is worth monitoring in production: a rise above the calibration level means the corpus has drifted from
the query distribution, and the threshold should be recalibrated rather than the gate removed.

6.3 Limitations

     Labels. The sufficiency labels are not direct human sufficiency ratings but human-annotated supporting
facts (Section 4.5). Validated against human judgement on a 175-query subsample (100 HotpotQA, 75
MuSiQue), annotators judged 75.0% of HotpotQA and 46.7% of MuSiQue contexts sufficient, against 22.6%
and 5.0% under the supporting-fact criterion, so human judgement is markedly more lenient than either
automatic criterion. Pooled over the subsample, agreement is fair for supporting-fact coverage (Cohen's
 = 0.416) and moderate for answer-string containment ( = 0.568), but weaker on HotpotQA taken alone
( = 0.300 and 0.358). Supporting-fact coverage is retained because it is deterministic, reproducible from
the datasets' own annotations and conservative, but no criterion achieves strong agreement with human
judgement, and results in this literature should be read as conditional on the criterion used.

     Statistical scope. The testing addresses selection rather than effect size. The permutation test establishes
whether a gate withholds queries better than chance and the bootstrap intervals whether one scorer ranks
contexts better than another; neither establishes that the resulting reduction is large enough to matter
operationally. On MuSiQue the LLM-judge clears the control at p = 0.0034 while reducing the conditional
unsupported rate by only 3.8 points, and those two facts should be weighed together.

     Measurement. The hallucination measure is automated, not human verified. Entailment models may
miss valid paraphrases or be misled by answers appearing in unrelated contexts, so both false alarms and
missed hallucinations are possible; this is why an unsupported-answer rate is reported rather than a direct
count of hallucinations. Establishing the measure's precision would require supportedness annotation on a
further subsample.

     Corpus size. The 5000-passage corpus is small compared with production deployments, and Section 5.8
shows how much this constrains the study: complete gold evidence is present in the index for only 52.4% of
HotpotQA and 31.6% of MuSiQue queries (again an index-coverage rate, distinct from the HotpotQA k = 6
sufficiency share of 31.6%). This caps what any gate can achieve, it is the main reason the re-retrieve variant
recovers so little, and it means the absolute rates should not be read as estimates of what the same gates would
do over a corpus that contains the answers.

     Scorer design. None of the scorers tested estimates collective sufficiency in the sense the task requires.
The heuristic has no representation of what several passages contribute jointly, and single-passage cross-
encoder scoring takes a maximum and is therefore structurally blind to evidence spread across passages.
Set-level scoring and the LLM-judge see the concatenated context and can in principle reason over it, but
Comput Mater Contin. 2026  33

both are still trained or prompted for a different objective than the one being measured. A scorer trained
directly on multi-hop sufficiency labels remains untested here.

     Generator scale. Flan-T5-Large is small by current standards, so the evaluation was repeated with
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Section 5.9). At both scales the scorer ordering sur-
vives: the LLM-judge remains best, the cross-encoders follow, and the heuristic stays near the ungated
baseline. Absolute unsupported rates still shift with the generator, and whether the same ordering holds
above 7B is not established here.

     Truncation asymmetry. Max-CE, Concat-CE and the LLM-judge use different character budgets
(512/1500/2000). A shared-budget sweep shows the main ranking is stable at the paper's operating lengths,
while a short shared budget (L = 512) weakens the judge on MuSiQue. The asymmetry is therefore disclosed
as a design choice tied to each scorer's input format, not claimed to be irrelevant.

     External validity. All evaluation is on English text: Wikipedia-derived multi-hop QA (HotpotQA,
MuSiQue), plus MS MARCO web passages and the unanswerable split of SQuAD 2.0 (Sections 5.10 and 5.11).
The out-of-domain runs use Contriever retrieval at k = 6 rather than the MiniLM retriever of the main tables,
so absolute rates are not compared across pipelines. The judge's calibrated threshold does not transfer, and
these data cannot separate appropriately conservative behaviour from miscalibration on an unfamiliar score
distribution. Deploying outside the calibration domain would therefore require re-calibrating  on a small
held-out sample from the target domain, which is not attempted here. Non-English text, non-encyclopedic
writing and larger corpora remain untested.

     Self-RAG comparison scope. The controlled comparison (Section 5.12) runs at one model scale on one
dataset, so it does not establish whether the parity it returns holds at Self-RAG's original 7B scale (Section 5.9)
or on HotpotQA. On the matched 357 queries the gate answers, the interval is wide enough to be consistent
with either method being modestly better on hallucination.

6.4 Future Work

     Four directions follow directly from the findings above, each addressing a specific limit encoun-
tered here.

     Separate joint encoding from the training objective. Set-level scoring lets the scorer see the whole context,
but the model is still trained for relevance, so this design cannot say which factor produced the +0.100
AUROC gain: the set-level and single-passage scorers share an objective, while the judge differs in encoding
and objective at once. A scorer trained directly on multi-hop sufficiency labels would isolate it.

     Give re-retrieval somewhere to look. Expansion recovered almost nothing because the missing evidence
is frequently absent from the index at any depth. Routing an insufficiency signal to live search rather than to
a refusal would test the mechanism where the evidence can actually be found.

     Make the operating point per-query. The threshold is currently one fixed percentile for all queries.
Adapting it to an estimate of what a wrong answer would cost on that particular query is the natural extension
of the risk­coverage view.

     Separate "no evidence" from "not sure". The metric asks whether an answer is entailed by its context,
which is weaker than asking whether it is correct. Pairing the gate with a calibrated correctness estimate
would let a system distinguish refusing because the evidence is missing from refusing because the model is
uncertain--two cases that warrant different fallbacks.

7 Conclusion

     We have studied the Sufficiency Gate, a module placed between the retriever and the generator that
estimates whether the retrieved context is adequate before generation is invoked, and we have measured
what it delivers when nothing in the pipeline is trained. The central result is a correction to how such gates
are scored. Counting abstentions as non-hallucinations takes the unsupported-answer rate from 26.2% to as
little as 9.8% on HotpotQA and from 30.8% to 13.6% on MuSiQue, but measured over the answers a system
actually gives, the best gate reaches 17.1% and 27.0%, and the heuristic that appears to more than halve the
MuSiQue rate is in fact worse than not gating at all (37.0% conditional against 30.8%). Reporting conditional
rates against a random abstainer withholding at the same rate is what separates selection from accounting.

     What survives that test is specific. Of the four scorers compared under one protocol, only the generator
prompted as a sufficiency judge beats its matched random control on both datasets, reaching 17.2% against
26.2% at 57.0% coverage on HotpotQA and 27.0% against 30.8% at 71.0% coverage on MuSiQue, and it leads at
every threshold by area under the risk­coverage curve. Scoring the retrieved set jointly rather than taking its
best single passage is the largest single design change we identify, worth +0.100 AUROC on HotpotQA. The
training-free heuristic fails outright, and the query-performance predictors that retrieval already computes
rank sufficiency well without surviving as gates. An oracle-context substitution removes roughly two thirds of
answer-quality failure and between a third and a half of unsupported answers, so a gate placed here addresses
the largest single source of failure even though the gains available without training are modest. The ordering
of the scorers replicates on a second generator; the absolute levels belong to this corpus and should not be
read as estimates for a larger one.

     The contribution is therefore a measurement rather than a mechanism: the untrained ceiling
of sufficiency-guided selective generation, together with the protocol that makes such measurements
interpretable--conditional rather than credited rates, a matched random-abstention control, a permutation
test on selection, and risk­coverage curves in place of a single operating point. On the evidence here, the
most valuable component of a sufficiency gate is not the gate but the generator's own judgement of whether
it has been given enough to work with.
